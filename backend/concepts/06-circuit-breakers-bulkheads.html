Circuit Breakers and Bulkheads

Circuit breaker states (closed/open/half-open) and required fallback behavior, bulkheads as resource partitioning to limit blast radius, and the supporting cast: timeouts, jittered backoff, retry budgets.

Theory

Both are failure isolation

Circuit breakers and bulkheads are both failure isolation patterns. They answer the same question from two different angles: when one dependency is broken, how do we stop it from taking down everything else? A bulkhead stops a broken dependency from starving unrelated code paths. A circuit breaker stops requests from continuing to hit a dependency that's already known to be broken. You need both, and naming both — plus the relationship between them — is what separates a senior answer from a definitions recital.

Circuit breaker

A circuit breaker wraps a call to a dependency and tracks its failure rate over a rolling window. It has three states:

  • Closed — normal operation. Calls pass through to the dependency. Failures are counted.
  • Open — the failure threshold was crossed. Calls fail immediately without attempting the dependency. This is the whole point: you stop wasting threads, connections, and time on something you already know is broken.
  • Half-open — after a cooldown, allow a small number of trial calls through. If they succeed, close the circuit and resume normal traffic. If they fail, reopen and extend the cooldown (exponential backoff on the breaker itself, not just the caller).

Why it matters more than retries: retrying a struggling service makes it worse, not better — it adds load to something that's already failing under load. A circuit breaker is the mechanism that stops a slow dependency from becoming a total outage: without one, every request thread blocks waiting on the failing call, the thread pool exhausts, and a single broken downstream takes out an unrelated endpoint running on the same service, purely because they shared a thread pool.

What to configure: the failure threshold (a rate, not an absolute count — 5 failures out of 5 calls means something very different from 5 failures out of 5,000), the window it's measured over, the cooldown duration, and — most important and most often skipped — the fallback. An open circuit needs a defined behavior: a cached value, a sane default, a degraded response, or an explicit error surfaced to the caller. A circuit breaker with no fallback doesn't fix anything, it just fails faster and more cheaply.

Timeouts are the prerequisite. A call with no timeout can't be detected as "failed" — it just hangs, and a circuit breaker has nothing to count. Every remote call needs a timeout, and that timeout needs to be meaningfully shorter than the caller's own deadline, or the breaker trips too late to matter.

Bulkhead

Named after ship compartments: a hull breach floods one compartment, not the vessel. In software, a bulkhead partitions resources so exhaustion in one area can't starve another. Forms this takes in practice:

Per-dependency pools

Separate thread pools or connection pools per downstream dependency. If the slow service has its own pool of 10 threads, it can exhaust those 10 and nothing else — the other 90 threads in the fleet keep serving unrelated traffic.

Per-tenant / per-priority pools

Separate pools per tenant or priority class in a multi-tenant system. One noisy or abusive tenant's burst can't consume capacity that belongs to everyone else.

Separate instances

For genuinely critical paths, dedicate entire instances rather than sharing a fleet — the strongest (and most expensive) form of isolation.

The relationship between the two patterns: the bulkhead limits the blast radius, the circuit breaker shortens the duration. A bulkhead caps how much damage a broken dependency can do at any instant; a circuit breaker caps how long it keeps doing it. They're complementary, not substitutes for each other — a bulkhead without a breaker still burns its whole partition on every request; a breaker without a bulkhead still lets one dependency's blocked threads come from a shared pool that starves everything else until the breaker trips.

Related patterns worth knowing

  • Timeout — the foundation underneath all of this. Nothing else works without it.
  • Retry with exponential backoff and jitter — jitter is essential, not decorative. Without it, every failed client backs off on the same schedule and retries in lockstep, producing synchronized load spikes that look like a second outage.
  • Retry budget — cap retries as a percentage of total traffic (e.g. no more than 10% of requests may be retries) so retries can't amplify a partial outage into a full one.
  • Graceful degradation — complete the operation without the failed component, and say so explicitly. Better than failing outright, provided the degradation is visible to whoever consumes the result, not silently swallowed.
  • Fail fast vs. fail safe — decide per dependency which is correct. Some failures should abort the whole operation (payment authorization); some should be absorbed and the operation should proceed in a degraded mode (a recommendations widget).

Where this appeared in the Simbian design: threat intel gets a circuit breaker with a defined fallback — when it's open, the investigation completes without reputation data and the verdict is marked degraded with the missing evidence named. Silently omitting it would be dangerous: a lowered-confidence verdict is honest, a confident one built on partial evidence isn't.

Key Takeaways

  • The bulkhead limits the blast radius, the circuit breaker shortens the duration — they're complementary isolation patterns, not alternatives. Naming both, and the relationship between them, is what signals you've actually operated a system that fell over this way.
  • A circuit breaker with no fallback just fails faster — the fallback (cached value, default, degraded response, explicit error) is the actual point of tripping the breaker, not the state machine itself.
  • Timeouts are the prerequisite for everything here — a call with no timeout can't be detected as failed, it just hangs and holds a thread forever, which is exactly what the bulkhead is trying to prevent.
  • Retries only make sense against idempotent operations — without idempotency (see 05-idempotency), a "helpful" retry after a circuit breaker half-opens is just a second bug, not a safety net. Idempotency is what makes retries safe, which is what makes circuit breakers useful rather than lossy.
  • Jitter on backoff isn't optional — synchronized retries from thousands of clients hitting the same schedule produce a load spike that looks like a second outage; a retry budget (cap retries at, say, 10% of total traffic) stops retries from amplifying a partial outage into a full one.
  • Per-dependency thread/connection pools are the same "one bad actor can't drain shared capacity" instinct behind rate limiting and backpressure (02-queues-backpressure, 08-rate-limiting) — applied to internal resource contention instead of external request volume.
