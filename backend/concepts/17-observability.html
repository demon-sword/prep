Observability (Metrics, Logs, Traces)

The three pillars — metrics, logs, and distributed traces — plus SLIs/SLOs/SLAs and error budgets, and how they compose to answer "is the system healthy" and "why did this one request fail."

Theory

Three pillars, three different questions

"Observability" gets used as a single word for three tools that answer three different questions, and the interview tell is whether you can say which tool answers which. Metrics are numeric time series — counters, gauges, histograms — aggregated cheaply at collection time, and they answer "is the system healthy right now, in aggregate." They're what dashboards and alerts are built on because they're cheap to store and query at any retention window. Logs are discrete, timestamped, high-fidelity records of individual events, and they answer "why did this specific thing happen" — the full context (stack trace, request payload, user ID) that a metric can't afford to carry because carrying it for every event would be too expensive to store. Traces are the request's-eye view: a single request's journey through every service it touched, broken into spans (one span per unit of work — an RPC call, a DB query, a cache lookup), and they answer "where in this one request did the time go." None of the three replaces the others. A metric tells you p99 latency on checkout spiked at 14:02. A trace for one of those slow requests tells you the time went into the payment service. A log line from the payment service tells you it's retrying because a downstream TLS cert expired. You need the metric to know something's wrong, the trace to know where, and the log to know why — that chain is the whole point of running all three.

Metrics: types, cardinality, and the aggregation trap

Metric systems (Prometheus, Datadog, CloudWatch) expose three real shapes: a counter (monotonically increasing, e.g. requests_total), a gauge (goes up and down, e.g. queue_depth), and a histogram (a distribution bucketed into ranges, used to derive percentiles like p50/p99 latency — never average a latency metric, because an average hides the tail that's actually hurting users). The trap that bites in production is cardinality: every unique combination of label values (endpoint, status_code, customer_id) creates a new time series, and a well-meaning engineer who tags a metric with user_id can turn a metric with 50 series into one with 5 million, which is how a Prometheus instance runs out of memory or a Datadog bill goes from four figures to six. The fix is a cardinality budget — high-cardinality dimensions (user ID, request ID) belong in logs and traces, not metric labels.

Distributed tracing: trace IDs, spans, and sampling

A trace is stitched together by propagating a trace ID across every service boundary in the call — generated at the edge (API gateway) and forwarded in a header (traceparent under the W3C Trace Context standard, or a vendor-specific header) through every downstream RPC and HTTP call. Each service, on receiving the trace ID, opens one or more spans — a span has a start time, duration, a parent span ID (which is what lets a backend like Jaeger or Tempo reconstruct the parent-child tree into a waterfall), and arbitrary tags (HTTP status, DB query, error flag). Because tracing every request at 100% is expensive at real traffic volumes, production systems sample — head-based sampling decides at the start of a request whether to trace it at all (e.g. 1% of traffic, or 100% of anything already flagged slow), while tail-based sampling buffers spans and decides after the fact, which is what lets you keep 100% of error traces and slow traces while still dropping most of the boring, fast, successful ones. The real cost of sampling badly: a rare but important failure mode can simply not show up in the 1% you kept, which is why error and outlier requests usually get sampled at a much higher rate than the happy path.

SLIs, SLOs, SLAs, and the error budget

An SLI (service level indicator) is the actual measured metric — e.g. the fraction of requests served in under 300ms with a 2xx status. An SLO (objective) is the internal target for that SLI — "99.9% of requests succeed within 300ms, measured over a rolling 30-day window." An SLA (agreement) is the SLO's externally contractual, usually looser, cousin with financial penalties attached — teams set the SLO tighter than the SLA on purpose so they have room to notice and fix a degradation before it becomes a contractual breach. The error budget is what makes an SLO actionable instead of aspirational: budget = (1 − SLO) × window. A 99.9% SLO over a 30-day window gives you 0.1% × 43,200 minutes ≈ 43 minutes of allowed badness for the month. Spend it on planned risk — a risky deploy, a chaos experiment, a migration — and stop shipping risky changes once it's gone; that's the entire cultural function of an error budget, converting "don't break things" into a number both engineering and product can negotiate over.

The trade-off that actually matters: burn rate vs raw threshold alerting

The naive alert — "page if error rate > 1%" — either fires on noise (a single bad minute during a routine deploy) or misses a slow bleed that never crosses the threshold but still exhausts the whole month's budget by day 20. The fix used in practice (Google SRE's approach) is burn-rate alerting: compute how many multiples of the sustainable rate you're currently burning the budget at. A burn rate of 1 means you're on pace to exhaust the budget exactly at the end of the window — sustainable. A burn rate of 10 means you'll exhaust a 30-day budget in 3 days; a burn rate of 100 means you'll exhaust it in about 7 hours, which is exactly the kind of thing that should page someone immediately, on a short observation window (5–60 minutes), while a burn rate of 2 over a long window (6 hours–3 days) is a ticket, not a page. This is the same instinct as circuit breakers (06-circuit-breakers-bulkheads) tracking a rolling failure rate rather than a single failed call — you're smoothing a noisy signal into a decision that's actually worth waking someone up for.

Cardinality explosion

A high-cardinality label (user ID, request ID, raw URL with path params) on a metric multiplies the number of time series, which can OOM a Prometheus instance or blow up a vendor bill overnight — keep high-cardinality data in logs/traces, not metric labels.

Alert fatigue from raw thresholds

Alerting directly on "error rate > X%" without a burn-rate/time-window model either pages on noise or silently misses a slow multi-day budget bleed — both outcomes train the on-call to ignore or distrust the alert.

Under-sampled traces losing the incident

Fixed low-rate head-based sampling (e.g. flat 1%) can simply not capture the one rare failure mode you needed — mitigate with tail-based sampling that keeps errors and outliers at a much higher rate than the happy path.

Averaging a latency metric

Reporting mean latency instead of p50/p95/p99 hides exactly the tail that's causing user pain — a histogram-backed percentile is the only honest way to represent a skewed latency distribution.

Interview Line

"The three pillars answer different questions and you genuinely need distinct tools for each: metrics are cheap and aggregated and answer 'is the system healthy right now' — that's your dashboards and alerts; traces answer 'where in this one request did the time go' by propagating a trace ID across every service boundary and stitching spans back into a waterfall; logs answer 'why did this specific thing happen' with the full-fidelity detail a metric can't afford to keep. A metric tells you p99 spiked, a trace tells you it's the payment service, a log tells you it's retrying because a downstream cert expired — you need all three, not one. The other half is SLOs: an SLO isn't a target you hit for its own sake, it's what converts 'the error rate went up' into a number engineering and product can actually negotiate over. An error budget is just (1 − SLO) × window, and what actually pages someone is burn-rate — how many multiples of the sustainable rate you're consuming the budget at right now — not the raw error rate, because a flat threshold either fires on noise during a routine deploy or completely misses a slow bleed that quietly exhausts the whole month by day 20."

Key Takeaways

  • Match the tool to the question — "is it healthy" is a metric, "where did the time go" is a trace, "why did it fail" is a log; reaching for the wrong pillar (grepping logs to answer an aggregate health question) is the tell of an under-instrumented system.
  • Never average a latency metric — store it as a histogram and read p50/p95/p99; the average hides exactly the tail that's causing the pain, and a 10x p99 with a flat average is a real, common production shape.
  • Cardinality is the metrics equivalent of a hot partition (04-sharding-partitioning) — a high-cardinality label like user_id on a metric multiplies time series and can OOM Prometheus or blow up a vendor bill overnight; keep IDs in logs/traces, not metric labels.
  • Burn-rate alerting, not raw-threshold alerting — a short-window high burn rate (e.g. 14x over 1 hour) pages immediately, a long-window low burn rate (e.g. 2x over 6 hours) is a ticket; this mirrors circuit breakers (06-circuit-breakers-bulkheads) smoothing a rolling failure rate instead of tripping on one bad call.
  • Tail-based sampling beats flat-rate sampling for traces — keep 100% of error and outlier traces, drop most of the boring successful ones; a flat 1% sample can simply never capture the one rare failure mode you need to diagnose an incident.
  • SLO ≠ SLA on purpose — the internal SLO is deliberately tighter than the external, contractual SLA, so the team gets a warning and a chance to fix a degradation before it becomes a financially penalized breach.
