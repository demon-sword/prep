25Gradient Descent Variants

Batch, stochastic, and mini-batch gradient descent, plus momentum, RMSProp, and Adam — how each modifies the update rule to escape saddle points and speed convergence.

Theory

Batch, Stochastic, and Mini-Batch: Trading Gradient Quality for Speed

Vanilla ("batch") gradient descent computes the loss gradient over the entire training set before taking a single step: θ ← θ − η·∇L(θ; all data). It's the lowest-variance gradient estimate you can get, so its path down the loss surface is smooth, but for a dataset of a million rows it means a million-row forward+backward pass per parameter update — hopelessly slow, and it can't even fit in memory for large datasets. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) goes to the opposite extreme: one random example per update. Each step is cheap and the noise in the per-example gradient actually helps — it acts as an implicit regularizer and can kick the optimizer out of shallow local minima and saddle points — but the path is jagged and never fully settles (it oscillates around the minimum unless the learning rate is annealed). Mini-batch gradient descent is the production default: batches of 32–512 examples (commonly 32/64 for CNNs, up to several thousand for large-batch distributed training) balance gradient-estimate variance against hardware utilization — a GPU processes a batch of 256 about as fast as a batch of 32 because of parallelism, so larger batches (up to a point) give more signal per wall-clock second. The tradeoff: very large batches reduce gradient noise so much that generalization can suffer (the "generalization gap" observed with batch sizes beyond ~8K on ImageNet-scale training, per Keskar et al. 2016), which is why large-batch regimes pair with linear learning-rate scaling and warmup.

Momentum: An Exponential Moving Average of Gradients

Plain SGD treats every step independently, which is wasteful when the loss surface has a consistent downhill direction — it zig-zags across narrow ravines (steep in one direction, shallow in another) instead of moving efficiently along the ravine floor. Momentum fixes this by accumulating a velocity vector: v ← β·v + ∇L(θ), θ ← θ − η·v, with β typically 0.9 (meaning roughly the last 10 gradients are being averaged, weighted exponentially). Components of the gradient that agree step after step (the ravine-floor direction) constructively add up in v and accelerate; components that oscillate sign (the steep walls) cancel out and get damped. Nesterov momentum improves on this by evaluating the gradient at the "look-ahead" position θ − η·β·v rather than the current position — a small correction that gives meaningfully faster convergence in practice and is PyTorch's nesterov=True flag on SGD.

RMSProp and Adam: Per-Parameter Adaptive Learning Rates

A single global learning rate is a poor fit when different parameters have wildly different gradient scales (common in deep nets — embedding rows updated rarely need bigger steps than dense-layer weights updated every batch). AdaGrad was the first fix: divide each parameter's learning rate by the square root of the sum of all its squared past gradients — but that sum only grows, so the effective learning rate decays to zero and training stalls on long runs. RMSProp (Hinton, unpublished, 2012) replaces the running sum with an exponentially decaying average: s ← ρ·s + (1−ρ)·∇L(θ)², θ ← θ − η·∇L(θ)/(√s + ε), with decay ρ ≈ 0.9 and ε ≈ 1e-8 for numerical stability — this keeps the adaptive scaling responsive to recent gradient magnitude instead of decaying forever. Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2014) combines both ideas: it keeps an EMA of the gradient itself (first moment, like momentum, β1 = 0.9) and an EMA of the squared gradient (second moment, like RMSProp, β2 = 0.999), then applies bias correction (m̂ = m/(1−β1ᵗ), v̂ = v/(1−β2ᵗ)) because both EMAs are initialized at zero and are biased toward zero early in training. The update is θ ← θ − η·m̂/(√v̂ + ε). Adam's default learning rate is η = 0.001 in Keras/PyTorch, and it converges fast with little tuning, which is why it's the default optimizer for training transformers, GANs, and most research code.

Why This Matters at Saddle Points

In high-dimensional loss landscapes (millions of parameters), saddle points — not local minima — are the dominant type of critical point and the main obstacle to fast convergence (Dauphin et al., 2014, "Identifying and attacking the saddle point problem"). At a saddle, the gradient is near zero in at least one direction, so plain SGD crawls agonizingly slowly through the flat region — it only escapes because the direction is unstable (negative curvature), and the escape is exponentially slow, driven by x_t ≈ x_0·(1+ηλ)^t for a flat eigen-direction with curvature λ. Momentum escapes faster because it carries accumulated velocity through the flat region rather than restarting from a near-zero gradient every step. Adaptive methods (RMSProp/Adam) escape faster still because they explicitly divide by the RMS of recent gradients — in a low-gradient, low-curvature direction, that denominator is small, so the effective step size is larger exactly where SGD is stuck taking tiny steps.

Production Reality

Despite Adam's faster and more robust convergence, plain SGD with momentum still outperforms Adam on final test accuracy for many CNN benchmarks (Wilson et al., 2017, "The Marginal Value of Adaptive Gradient Methods in Machine Learning") — Adam's adaptive scaling can converge to sharper minima that generalize worse. This is why ResNet/ImageNet training recipes still commonly use SGD+momentum with a step or cosine learning-rate schedule, while transformer pretraining (BERT, GPT-family) almost universally uses AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) — Adam with weight decay decoupled from the gradient-adaptive update, fixing a subtle bug where naive L2 regularization interacts badly with Adam's per-parameter scaling. Production recipes pair Adam/AdamW with a learning-rate warmup (linear ramp over the first ~1–10K steps) because the second-moment estimate v is noisy and unreliable early in training when few gradients have been observed, causing instability at full learning rate. Common production defaults: AdamW at η ≈ 1e-4 to 6e-4 with a few thousand warmup steps for transformers; SGD+momentum at η ≈ 0.1 with a 90-epoch step decay for ImageNet CNNs. Gradient clipping (cap the global gradient norm at 1.0–5.0) is standard alongside any of these to protect against exploding gradients on rare pathological batches. Frameworks: torch.optim.SGD, torch.optim.Adam/AdamW, and tf.keras.optimizers implement all of the above out of the box.

Key Takeaways

  • Mini-batch size is a hardware/statistics tradeoff, not a free lunch: 32–512 is the practical sweet spot; batches beyond ~8K (large-batch distributed training) need linear LR scaling and warmup to avoid a documented generalization gap (Keskar et al., 2016).
  • Momentum and adaptive methods both help most at saddle points, not local minima — in high-dimensional nets, saddle points vastly outnumber local minima (Dauphin et al., 2014), and Adam/RMSProp's 1/√v scaling specifically boosts step size in the low-gradient directions where plain SGD stalls.
  • Adam converges faster but doesn't always generalize best — SGD+momentum with a cosine or step LR schedule still wins on final test accuracy for many CNN benchmarks (Wilson et al., 2017), which is why ImageNet training recipes still default to SGD while transformer pretraining defaults to AdamW.
  • Use AdamW, not Adam + L2 penalty, whenever you want weight decay — naive L2 regularization interacts incorrectly with Adam's per-parameter adaptive scaling (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017); PyTorch's torch.optim.AdamW decouples the two correctly.
  • Learning-rate warmup exists because Adam's second-moment estimate is unreliable in the first few hundred steps — production transformer runs ramp linearly over 1K–10K steps before hitting peak LR (typically 1e-4 to 6e-4) to avoid early instability.
  • Gradient clipping (global norm 1.0–5.0) is cheap insurance against exploding gradients on rare pathological batches, and is standard practice alongside any of these optimizers in RNN/transformer training where gradient magnitudes can spike unpredictably.
