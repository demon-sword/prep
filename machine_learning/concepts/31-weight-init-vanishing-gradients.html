31Weight Init & Vanishing Gradients

Why naive weight initialization causes vanishing/exploding activations and gradients, and how Xavier/He initialization keeps variance stable across layers.

Theory

Variance Propagation Through a Linear Layer

Consider a single fully-connected layer y = Wx where x has n_in independent, zero-mean components with variance Var(x), and W has independent zero-mean entries with variance Var(w). Each output y_i = Σ w_ij x_j is a sum of n_in independent zero-mean products, so its variance is Var(y) = n_in · Var(w) · Var(x). Stack L such layers with no normalization in between and the variance compounds multiplicatively across depth: Var(a_L) ≈ Var(a_0) · Π (n_in · Var(w_l)). If the per-layer factor n_in · Var(w) is even slightly above 1, activations blow up exponentially with depth; if it's slightly below 1, they shrink to zero exponentially. There is exactly one factor that keeps variance constant across arbitrary depth: n_in · Var(w) = 1, i.e. Var(w) = 1/n_in. This single identity is the entire derivation behind every "principled" initialization scheme — the schemes differ only in what they additionally account for (backward pass, saturating nonlinearities, ReLU's zeroing behavior).

Xavier/Glorot Initialization

Glorot & Bengio (2010) applied the same variance argument to the backward pass — gradients flowing through W^T compound by a factor of n_out · Var(w) — and noted you generally can't satisfy both Var(w) = 1/n_in and Var(w) = 1/n_out simultaneously unless the layer is square. Their compromise, now called Xavier or Glorot initialization, splits the difference: Var(w) = 2 / (n_in + n_out), commonly implemented as a uniform draw U(−√(6/(n_in+n_out)), +√(6/(n_in+n_out))) or a Gaussian with that variance. This derivation implicitly assumes a linear or near-linear activation around zero (true for tanh and sigmoid in their small-signal regime), and it works well empirically for tanh networks up to tens of layers — but it silently breaks for ReLU networks.

He Initialization — Accounting for ReLU

ReLU zeroes out roughly half of its inputs (every negative pre-activation), which exactly halves the variance passed forward compared to a linear layer. He et al. (2015) redid the variance derivation with this correction and arrived at Var(w) = 2/n_in — twice the Xavier variance for the same fan-in, exactly compensating for the factor of 2 that ReLU throws away. In their paper, a plain 22-layer ReLU CNN with Xavier init failed to converge at all (loss stayed flat), while the identical architecture with He init converged normally — a concrete demonstration that tanh-appropriate initialization is actively harmful once the activation function changes. The practical rule: match the init scheme to the activation function — Xavier/Glorot for tanh/sigmoid, He/Kaiming for ReLU and its variants (LeakyReLU, ELU with minor adjustment).

Two Failure Modes, One Root Cause

With tanh, over-scaled weights (Var(w) ≫ 1/n_in) don't just make activations numerically large — they push pre-activations into tanh's saturating tails, where the function is nearly flat and its derivative 1 − tanh²(z) is near zero. So an "exploding activations" forward pass frequently causes a vanishing gradient backward pass, because saturated units kill their own local gradient regardless of the weight magnitude multiplying them. Under-scaled weights (Var(w) ≪ 1/n_in) cause the more obvious failure: activations shrink toward zero every layer, so by layer 10–20 the entire forward signal is numerically indistinguishable from zero and every gradient computed from it is zero too. Both failure modes are visible directly in a per-layer activation-variance plot — collapsing toward 0 or diverging toward infinity — which is exactly what a well-chosen init keeps flat.

Production Reality

PyTorch's nn.Linear and nn.Conv2d default to a Kaiming-uniform variant (a=√5, fan_in mode) which in practice behaves close to U(−1/√n_in, 1/√n_in) — a reasonable ReLU-oriented default, but it's still worth explicitly calling nn.init.kaiming_normal_(w, nonlinearity='relu') or nn.init.xavier_normal_ when you know your activation function, especially for very deep or unusual architectures. Keras/TensorFlow defaults to glorot_uniform for Dense and Conv2D layers, which is the wrong choice if you're stacking ReLU — Keras exposes he_normal explicitly for this reason. Batch Normalization and Layer Normalization dramatically reduce sensitivity to initialization by renormalizing activations at every layer regardless of the incoming weight scale, which is a large part of why 100+ layer networks became trainable without hand-tuned init — but init still matters in the first few steps before running statistics stabilize, and matters a lot in normalization-free architectures (some Transformer variants, RL policy networks). Residual connections (ResNet, Transformers) provide an *additive* identity path around each block, so even if a block's own weights are poorly scaled, the gradient has a clean unimpeded route back through the skip connection — this is why ResNets tolerate depth (152+ layers) that plain feedforward CNNs never could regardless of init. For extremely deep or unusual nets, LSUV (Layer-Sequential Unit-Variance, Mishkin & Matas 2015) initializes with orthogonal matrices then rescales each layer using a mini-batch forward pass so output variance is empirically ≈1, and modern large-scale Transformer training often applies additional depth-dependent scaling (e.g. GPT-2's residual-projection weights scaled by 1/√(2·n_layers)) precisely because compounding variance across 50+ residual blocks is still a real risk even with residual paths.

Key Takeaways

  • The entire theory reduces to one identity: for a linear layer, Var(output) = n_in · Var(w) · Var(input), so keeping variance constant across depth requires Var(w) = 1/n_in — Xavier and He are both just refinements of this single equation for different activation functions and both forward/backward passes.
  • Match the init scheme to the activation, not just "use a good default" — He et al. (2015) showed a 22-layer plain ReLU CNN with Xavier init fails to converge entirely (flat loss), while the same net with He init (σ² = 2/n_in, accounting for ReLU zeroing half its inputs) trains normally. PyTorch's nn.Linear default and Keras' glorot_uniform default are not automatically correct for every activation you attach.
  • "Exploding forward activations" and "vanishing gradients" are often the same bug — over-scaled weights push tanh/sigmoid units into their saturating tails, where the local derivative is near zero, so the forward signal looks large while the backward gradient dies anyway.
  • BatchNorm/LayerNorm reduce, but don't eliminate, init sensitivity — normalization renormalizes activations every layer regardless of incoming weight scale, which is why 100+ layer nets became practically trainable, but init still governs the first few optimizer steps before running statistics stabilize, and matters a lot in norm-free architectures.
  • Residual connections are as much an init fix as an architecture choice — the additive skip path gives gradients a route back that bypasses any single block's weight scale, which is why ResNet-152 and 96-layer Transformers train at all; large-scale Transformer training still applies explicit depth-dependent down-scaling of residual-branch weights (e.g. GPT-2's 1/√(2·n_layers) factor) because 50+ stacked residual blocks can still compound variance.
  • LSUV and data-dependent init exist for cases where closed-form variance math isn't enough — Mishkin & Matas (2015) initialize with orthogonal matrices, then rescale each layer using an actual mini-batch forward pass so measured output variance ≈ 1; useful for unusual architectures where the analytical Xavier/He derivation's assumptions (independent zero-mean weights and inputs) don't cleanly hold.
