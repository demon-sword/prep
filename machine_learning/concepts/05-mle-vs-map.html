05MLE vs MAP Estimation

Maximum likelihood estimation vs maximum a posteriori estimation, and how a prior regularizes MLE — the probabilistic view of L2 regularization.

Theory

MLE: Let the Data Speak

Maximum likelihood estimation picks the parameter value θ that makes the observed data most probable, treating θ as a fixed unknown constant (the frequentist view — no probability distribution over parameters, only over data). Given i.i.d. observations x₁, …, x_n with likelihood p(x_i | θ), MLE solves:

θ̂_MLE = argmax_θ  ∏ᵢ p(xᵢ|θ) = argmax_θ  Σᵢ log p(xᵢ|θ)

Taking logs turns the product into a sum — numerically stable and the reason every ML loss you've ever minimized (cross-entropy, squared error) is secretly a negative log-likelihood. For a Gaussian with known variance σ², maximizing the log-likelihood over μ is algebraically identical to minimizing squared error, which is why OLS linear regression is MLE under a Gaussian-noise assumption, and why cross-entropy loss for classification is MLE under a Bernoulli/categorical likelihood. MLE has strong asymptotic guarantees: it is consistent (converges to the true parameter as n→∞), asymptotically normal, and asymptotically efficient (achieves the Cramér-Rao lower bound — the lowest possible variance among unbiased estimators) — but all of that is asymptotic. With small n, MLE has no built-in mechanism to avoid overfitting to noise; it will happily assign zero probability to unseen categories (the classic "unseen word in Naive Bayes" failure) or blow up in the presence of near-separable classes in logistic regression.

MAP: Let the Data Speak, But Start With an Opinion

Maximum a posteriori estimation is the Bayesian correction: treat θ itself as a random variable with a prior distribution p(θ) encoding belief before seeing data, then pick the mode of the posterior:

θ̂_MAP = argmax_θ  p(θ|X) = argmax_θ  p(X|θ)·p(θ) = argmax_θ  [Σᵢ log p(xᵢ|θ) + log p(θ)]

By Bayes' theorem, the posterior is proportional to likelihood × prior (the normalizing evidence term p(X) doesn't depend on θ so it drops out of the argmax). The prior term log p(θ) acts exactly like an additive penalty on the log-likelihood — which is the entire probabilistic justification for regularization. If θ has a Gaussian prior N(0, τ²), then log p(θ) = −θ²/(2τ²) + const, and maximizing the log-posterior is identical to minimizing negative log-likelihood plus an L2 penalty — ridge regression is MAP estimation with a Gaussian prior, and the regularization strength λ is inversely proportional to the prior variance τ² (a tight prior, small τ, means strong regularization). If instead θ has a Laplace prior (heavier-tailed, sharply peaked at 0), log p(θ) ∝ −|θ|/b, and MAP becomes exactly Lasso (L1) regression — the L1 penalty's tendency to zero out coefficients is the posterior mode of a Laplace prior concentrating mass at exactly zero.

The Conjugate Gaussian Case (What the Viz Shows)

For a Gaussian likelihood with known variance σ² and a Gaussian prior N(μ₀, τ₀²) on the mean, the posterior is also exactly Gaussian (conjugacy) with a closed form:

μ_MAP = (σ²·μ₀ + n·τ₀²·x̄) / (σ² + n·τ₀²)

This is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean μ₀ and the sample mean x̄ — the weights are the prior precision 1/τ₀² and the data precision n/σ². As n→∞ or τ₀→∞ (uninformative prior), μ_MAP → x̄ = μ_MLE: with enough data, the prior's influence vanishes and MAP converges to MLE. As τ₀→0 (a dogmatic prior), μ_MAP → μ₀ regardless of data. This is the essential intuition for every regularized estimator: the prior/penalty matters most when data is scarce and fades as data accumulates.

What MAP Doesn't Give You

MAP is still a point estimate — it reports the single most probable parameter value but discards the shape of the posterior (its width, skew, multimodality). A fully Bayesian treatment integrates over the entire posterior to get predictive distributions (p(x_new|X) = ∫p(x_new|θ)p(θ|X)dθ), which is what tools like PyMC, Stan, or variational inference in Pyro/NumPyro actually compute — MAP is the cheap approximation you fall back on when full posterior inference is computationally intractable (e.g., in deep networks, where MAP training with weight decay is the norm and full Bayesian neural nets remain a research niche).

Production Reality

In practice almost nobody says "I'm doing MAP estimation" — they say "I added L2 regularization" or "I set alpha=1.0 in sklearn.linear_model.Ridge." But the mapping is exact: scikit-learn's Ridge(alpha=λ) solves the same optimization as MAP with prior variance τ² = σ²/λ, and Lasso(alpha=λ) is MAP under a Laplace prior with scale b = σ²/λ. This reframing is genuinely useful in interviews and in practice: it explains why regularization strength should scale with how much you trust your prior belief relative to your data volume — with 10 data points and known domain knowledge (e.g. "conversion rates rarely exceed 5%"), a strong prior via MAP/regularization is a rational Bayesian update, not just a hack to prevent overfitting. It's also why hyperparameter tuning of λ via cross-validation is implicitly searching over "how much do I trust this prior," and why empirical Bayes methods estimate the prior's hyperparameters directly from data via marginal likelihood maximization instead of cross-validation grid search.

Key Takeaways

  • Every standard loss function is a negative log-likelihood in disguise. Squared error ⇔ Gaussian noise MLE; cross-entropy ⇔ Bernoulli/categorical MLE; Poisson deviance ⇔ Poisson MLE. Knowing this tells you exactly which noise/error assumption you're implicitly making about your target variable, and when it's violated (e.g. heavy-tailed residuals mean squared error is the wrong MLE objective — consider a Huber loss, which corresponds to a different noise model).
  • Ridge = MAP with a Gaussian prior; Lasso = MAP with a Laplace prior. sklearn.linear_model.Ridge(alpha=λ) and Lasso(alpha=λ) are literally computing posterior modes; λ is inversely proportional to prior variance. This is why doubling λ is equivalent to halving your prior's belief in large coefficients — a useful mental model for choosing a search range in GridSearchCV.
  • MAP's prior effect vanishes with data volume. The closed-form posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of prior and sample mean; with n in the thousands, a mildly informative prior barely moves the estimate. Priors matter most exactly where MLE is weakest: small-n regimes, rare categories, cold-start recommendation, and few-shot settings — which is why Bayesian smoothing (e.g. add-one/Laplace smoothing in Naive Bayes) is a MAP estimate with a Dirichlet prior.
  • MAP is still a point estimate — it throws away uncertainty. Two very different posteriors (a sharp unimodal one vs. a wide bimodal one) can share the same mode. If you need calibrated uncertainty (credible intervals, active learning acquisition functions, risk-aware decisions), you need the full posterior via MCMC (PyMC, Stan) or variational inference (NumPyro), not just its argmax.
  • MLE can fail catastrophically with sparse data. Zero-count categories get zero probability under plain MLE (breaks Naive Bayes entirely on unseen words), and logistic regression MLE diverges to infinite weights under perfectly separable classes. A weak prior/regularizer (MAP) fixes both — this is precisely why sklearn's LogisticRegression has L2 regularization on by default (C=1.0), not because it's "extra safety" but because unregularized MLE is often ill-posed.
  • Empirical Bayes tunes the prior itself from data — maximizing the marginal likelihood p(X) = ∫p(X|θ)p(θ)dθ over prior hyperparameters instead of cross-validating λ by grid search. This is how Gaussian Process regression libraries (GPflow, scikit-learn's GaussianProcessRegressor) automatically select kernel lengthscales without a held-out validation set.
