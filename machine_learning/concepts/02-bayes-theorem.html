02Bayes' Theorem

Prior, likelihood, and posterior — how Bayes' theorem updates beliefs given evidence, and why it underlies Naive Bayes, A/B testing, spam filters, and model calibration.

Theory

The Formula

Bayes' theorem is a one-line consequence of the definition of conditional probability, but its implications run through almost every probabilistic model in ML. For a hypothesis H and observed evidence E:

P(H | E)  =  P(E | H) · P(H)  /  P(E)

Each term has a name that matters for how you reason about it: P(H) is the prior — what you believed about the hypothesis before seeing evidence. P(E | H) is the likelihood — how probable this evidence is, assuming the hypothesis is true. P(H | E) is the posterior — your updated belief after seeing the evidence. P(E) is the evidence (or marginal likelihood) — a normalizing constant equal to Σ_h P(E | h)P(h) summed over all hypotheses, ensuring the posterior integrates to 1. In practice you rarely compute P(E) directly; you compute the numerator for every candidate hypothesis and normalize, or you just compare unnormalized posteriors (this is exactly what Naive Bayes classifiers do — see concept 11).

The Intuition That Trips People Up

The single most common Bayes' theorem mistake — in interviews and in production — is confusing P(E | H) with P(H | E). A test with 99% sensitivity (P(positive | disease) = 0.99) sounds like it means "99% of positive results are correct," but that's a different, unrelated number: P(disease | positive). When the disease is rare, the posterior can be shockingly low even with a highly accurate test, because the false positives from the enormous healthy population swamp the true positives from the tiny sick population. This is the "base rate fallacy," and it's precisely why the prior term in the numerator matters as much as the likelihood — a strong likelihood ratio can still lose to an extreme prior.

Odds Form — the Version Practitioners Actually Use

Working with raw probabilities requires computing the normalizer P(E), which is often intractable for continuous or high-dimensional hypotheses. The odds form sidesteps this entirely by taking a ratio of two applications of Bayes' theorem, which cancels P(E):

posterior odds  =  likelihood ratio  ×  prior odds

This is the working equation behind sequential probability updating: every new piece of independent evidence multiplies your current odds by that evidence's likelihood ratio. It's also why log-odds (logits) are the natural unit for Bayesian updating — multiplying likelihood ratios becomes adding log-likelihood-ratios, which is exactly the structure logistic regression and naive Bayes classifiers exploit internally.

Conjugate Priors and Sequential Updating

When the prior distribution and the likelihood function belong to a matching family, the posterior has a closed form and updating is just arithmetic on the distribution's parameters — no numerical integration needed. The canonical example: a Beta(α, β) prior over a Bernoulli/Binomial success probability θ, updated with k successes out of n trials, yields a posterior of Beta(α + k, β + n − k). This is the exact mechanism behind Thompson Sampling in multi-armed bandits, and it's what the interactive coin-flip visualization below computes live: each observed flip just increments α or β by 1.

Where This Shows Up in ML Systems

  • Naive Bayes classifiers apply Bayes' theorem with a conditional-independence assumption across features, turning a joint likelihood into a product of per-feature likelihoods (concept 11).
  • A/B testing — Bayesian A/B frameworks (as opposed to frequentist p-value testing) treat conversion rate as a random variable with a prior, updating to a posterior after observing conversions, and reporting P(treatment > control) directly instead of a p-value (concept 36).
  • Model calibration — a well-calibrated classifier's output should equal P(y=1 | x), a posterior probability. Platt scaling and isotonic regression are post-hoc corrections when a model's raw scores (e.g. an SVM's distance to the margin) aren't already posteriors.
  • Bayesian hyperparameter optimization (e.g. Gaussian-process-based tools like Optuna's TPE sampler) treats the objective function's value as evidence updating a posterior belief about where the optimum lies.
  • Spam filters, medical diagnostics, fraud detection — anywhere you combine a rare-event prior with noisy evidence, Bayes' theorem is the correct way to combine them, and naive "just look at the accuracy" reasoning will mislead you exactly as in the medical-test example below.

Production Reality

The base-rate fallacy is not an academic curiosity — it's the reason fraud and anomaly detection systems built on "99% accurate" models still need human review queues. If fraud is 0.1% of transactions and your classifier has 95% recall and 98% specificity, the vast majority of raised alerts are still false positives, because 2% of the 99.9% legitimate traffic dwarfs the true fraud caught. Teams that don't reason in Bayesian terms end up either drowning analysts in false alerts or under-trusting a genuinely good model. The fix is always the same: report precision (which already folds in the prior) alongside recall, not just sensitivity/specificity in isolation — see concept 22 for the full confusion-matrix breakdown.

Key Takeaways

  • Sensitivity/specificity are not the posterior you care about. A test can be 95% sensitive and 90% specific and still produce a posterior P(disease | positive) under 10% when prevalence is 1% — the false positives from the 99% healthy population outnumber true positives from the 1% sick population. Always ask "what's the prior?" before trusting a headline accuracy number.
  • Odds form avoids computing the normalizer. Production Bayesian systems (spam filters, Naive Bayes, Thompson sampling bandits) almost never compute P(E) directly — they track unnormalized log-odds and only normalize at the end, which is also numerically more stable for many small probabilities multiplied together.
  • Conjugate priors turn Bayesian updating into O(1) arithmetic. Beta-Binomial (used above), Gamma-Poisson, and Normal-Normal pairs let you update a posterior with simple parameter increments instead of numerical integration — this is why Thompson Sampling scales to millions of bandit arms in real ad-serving and recommendation systems.
  • Bayesian A/B testing reports P(B beats A) directly instead of a p-value — tools like PyMC or purpose-built frameworks (VWO's Bayesian engine, Optimizely's newer stats engine) let you monitor an experiment continuously without the "peeking" problem that inflates false-positive rates under frequentist testing (concept 36).
  • A narrow prior needs a lot of evidence to move. In the coin-flip demo, α₀=β₀=20 (a confident, sharply-peaked prior at θ=0.5) barely budges after 10 flips of pure heads; α₀=β₀=2 (a weak, near-uniform prior) swings hard. This is the probabilistic version of L2 regularization strength in MAP estimation (concept 05) — prior strength is a knob you set deliberately, not an afterthought.
  • Calibration ≠ discrimination. A model can rank examples perfectly (high AUC) while its raw output scores are terrible estimates of P(y=1|x) — gradient-boosted trees and SVMs are notorious for this. Platt scaling / isotonic regression (CalibratedClassifierCV in scikit-learn) fits a secondary Bayesian-flavored correction specifically to fix this gap before you use scores as decision-theoretic probabilities.
