03Central Limit Theorem

Why sample means of any distribution with finite variance approach a normal distribution as sample size grows, and why this fact underlies confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and the entire frequentist statistics toolkit.

Theory

The Statement

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) says that if you draw n independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables X₁, ..., Xₙ from any population with mean μ and finite variance σ², then the sampling distribution of the sample mean X̄ₙ = (1/n)Σ Xᵢ converges to a normal distribution as n → ∞ — regardless of the shape of the original population distribution. Formally (the Lindeberg–Lévy form):

√n · (X̄ₙ − μ) / σ  →d  N(0, 1)  as n → ∞

Equivalently, for finite n, X̄ₙ is approximately N(μ, σ²/n). The quantity σ/√n is the standard error — it shrinks with √n, not n, which is why cutting estimation error in half requires roughly 4× the data, not 2×. This single fact is the mathematical backbone of confidence intervals, z-tests, t-tests, and the Gaussian approximations used everywhere in applied statistics — including the A/B significance testing covered next (concept 04).

Why It's Not Magic — The Mechanism

The intuition is that summing many independent sources of variation causes their individual quirks (skew, multimodality, heavy tails) to cancel out on average while the aggregate's fluctuations settle into the one universal "bell" shape that emerges from adding up lots of small, independent perturbations. Characteristic-function (Fourier) proofs make this precise: the characteristic function of a sum of i.i.d. variables is the *product* of individual characteristic functions, and a second-order Taylor expansion of that product converges to the characteristic function of a Gaussian, independent of the original distribution's shape — the shape only enters through the mean and variance.

How Fast Is "Fast"? Skew Matters

The oft-cited "n ≥ 30 is enough" rule of thumb is a rough heuristic that badly under-serves skewed or heavy-tailed populations. The Berry–Esseen theorem bounds the rate of convergence: the maximum gap between the true CDF of the standardized mean and the normal CDF is O(ρ / (σ³√n)), where ρ = E[|X − μ|³] is the third absolute moment. Populations with large skew or heavy tails (transaction amounts, latency measurements, income data, click-through counts) have a large ρ relative to σ³, so they need a much larger n before the normal approximation is trustworthy — sometimes n in the thousands, not 30. A symmetric, light-tailed distribution (uniform, moderately-spread binomial) converges almost immediately, often visually "normal" by n=5.

Conditions and Where It Breaks

  • Finite variance is required. Distributions with infinite variance (e.g. Cauchy, or Pareto with shape parameter ≤ 2) never converge to a Gaussian no matter how large n gets — the sample mean's own distribution stays heavy-tailed. This matters in practice for power-law data like social-network follower counts or extreme financial returns.
  • i.i.d. is required — or at least a weak-dependence condition (martingale CLT, mixing conditions). Autocorrelated data (time series, clustered/panel data) violates independence and the naive standard error σ/√n understates the true uncertainty; you need block bootstrap, Newey–West standard errors, or hierarchical/clustered variance estimators instead.
  • Identically distributed is a convenience, not a strict requirement — the Lyapunov and Lindeberg CLT variants relax this to allow non-identical distributions under boundedness conditions, which is what lets CLT-style reasoning apply to sums of weighted, heterogeneous features (e.g. regression coefficients as weighted sums).

Where This Shows Up in ML Systems

CLT justifies treating estimated quantities as approximately Gaussian even when the underlying data isn't: A/B test conversion-rate differences, mean model loss across an evaluation set, bootstrap resampling of a metric (bootstrap works precisely because resampled statistics inherit CLT-like behavior), coefficient estimates in linear/logistic regression (asymptotic normality of MLE is a CLT consequence), and mini-batch gradient noise in SGD (averaging gradients over a batch is itself an application of CLT, which is why larger batch sizes produce lower-variance, more Gaussian-distributed gradient estimates).

Production Reality

Real production metrics are rarely nice: session duration, revenue-per-user, and API latency are right-skewed with heavy tails, often driven by a small number of high-value or pathological outliers. Teams that blindly apply a z-test or Welch's t-test with n=200 on heavily skewed revenue data can get miscalibrated p-values because Berry–Esseen convergence hasn't kicked in yet. The standard fixes: log-transform or winsorize the metric before testing, use the non-parametric bootstrap (which sidesteps distributional assumptions entirely and is what most modern experimentation platforms — Statsig, Eppo, internal Airbnb/Uber tooling — do under the hood), or increase sample size substantially past the naive n=30 heuristic. When in doubt, simulate: draw repeated samples from your actual historical metric distribution and check empirically how large n needs to be before the sampling distribution of the mean looks normal — exactly what the visualization below lets you do.

Key Takeaways

  • Standard error shrinks with √n, not n. Halving your confidence interval width requires roughly 4× the sample size. This is why "just collect a bit more data" is deceptively expensive once you're already past a few thousand samples — teams routinely underestimate this when scoping A/B test runtimes.
  • "n ≥ 30" is a heuristic for mild skew, not a law. Berry–Esseen convergence rate is O(ρ/(σ³√n)) — for heavy-tailed metrics like revenue-per-user or API latency (third-moment ρ large relative to σ³), you may need n in the thousands before a t-test's Gaussian assumption is trustworthy. Log-transforming or bootstrapping sidesteps this.
  • Infinite-variance distributions never converge. Cauchy-distributed data or Pareto with shape ≤ 2 (some fraud-loss and viral-content distributions look like this) break CLT outright — the sample mean itself is not a good estimator, and median-based or trimmed-mean statistics are more robust choices.
  • Bootstrap resampling is CLT in disguise. When you resample your dataset with replacement B times and compute a statistic each time, the resulting bootstrap distribution behaves like a sampling distribution and inherits approximate normality for large B — this is why scipy.stats.bootstrap and modern A/B platforms default to bootstrap CIs over closed-form formulas when metric distributions are unknown or skewed.
  • CLT justifies asymptotic normality of MLE. Regression coefficient standard errors, logistic regression Wald tests, and GLM confidence intervals all lean on the fact that maximum likelihood estimators are asymptotically normal around the true parameter — a direct CLT consequence, valid only in the large-sample regime (small-sample MLE inference needs profile likelihood or bootstrap instead).
  • Larger mini-batches produce lower-variance, more Gaussian gradient noise. SGD's per-batch gradient estimate is itself a sample mean over the batch — CLT is why bumping batch size from 32 to 256 measurably smooths the loss curve, at the cost of 8× the compute per step and often worse generalization (the "generalization gap" literature).
