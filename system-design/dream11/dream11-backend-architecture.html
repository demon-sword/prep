How 10 million ranks stay fresh — and how one of them gets paid

Two systems wearing one UI. Until the last ball, the leaderboard is eventually consistent and disposable: approximate, recomputable, cheap to throw away. The moment the match ends it becomes transactional, idempotent and auditable, because now it decides who gets ₹1 crore. The interesting engineering is the seam between them.

1 · Requirements & the constraints that shape everything

Three constraints do most of the design work. Say them out loud early in an interview.

The three constraints

1. Matches are scheduled. You know the exact minute traffic goes 30×. Capacity planning is a calendar problem, not an autoscaling problem — reactive HPA lags by minutes and minutes is the whole event.

2. Provider data is revised. Scorecard corrections, DLS, super overs, disputed catches. You cannot pay out on the MATCH_COMPLETE event.

3. Rosters freeze at lock. After the deadline, team composition is immutable — which is exactly what makes an in-memory compute engine safe and rebuildable.

  Q->>Q: rank = suffix[bucket+1] + 1
  Q-->>U: {rank, score, epoch 427}

Top-100 bytes are identical for every user → served from CDN at >95% hit rate. Only the personal rank touches origin.

Monotonicity beats freshness. A rank that reads 4000 → 3800 → 4000 across consecutive refreshes looks like a bug and generates support tickets. Every response carries epoch; the client echoes the highest it has seen as If-Epoch-At-Least; the server serves only epoch ≥ N. Bounded staleness is fine. Going backwards is not.

Add jitter to polling. A synchronised 30-second refresh across ten million clients is a self-inflicted DDoS every thirty seconds. Prefer SSE carrying only {"epoch":427} — "something changed, refetch from cache" — so the payload still comes from the CDN.

4 · The three tricks that make this cheap

The naive framing is "10 M teams × recompute on every ball = disaster." The real state is 40 integers.

4.1 — Don't score users, score players

A match has ≤ 40 players in the squad universe. That is the entire scoring state. A team is 11 players + captain (2×) + vice-captain (1.5×), so a team score is a dot product of a 40-slot points vector with a sparse multiplier vector. The expensive stage's output is 40 ints, not 10 M rows.

4.2 — Bit-pack the team, and de-duplication becomes free

Click players to build an XI, then set captain and vice-captain. Watch the 64-bit signature — two users who pick identically produce the same integer, so dedup is just a hash-map key.

Click a card to select/deselect (max 11). Use the C / V chips on a selected card to set captain and vice-captain.

A fantasy team is 8 bytes. Millions of users pick the same popular XI, so distinct signatures are 1–2 orders of magnitude fewer than teams: 10 M teams → ~500 k distinct signatures. You score the 500 k, then scatter.

4.3 — Rank with a histogram, not a sorted list

You do not need a sorted array of 10 M entries to answer "what is my rank?". Fantasy points are discrete and bounded, so bucket them and keep a suffix sum. Drag the slider — the rank below is computed the same way production would compute it.

my_rank   = suffix[my_bucket + 1] + 1   // O(1) — competition ranking

Histograms are mergeable. Shard teams across N nodes, build a local histogram on each, and the coordinator just adds the arrays element-wise. Top-K merges the same way. Both operations are associative, so the distributed merge is trivially correct — that is the whole reason this shape beats a sorted structure in a distributed system.

The epoch loop

func (s *Shard) RecomputeEpoch(pp *PlayerPoints) *Snapshot {
    // 1. dense 40-slot points vector, fixed point (×100)
    var pts [40]int32
    for _, p := range pp.Points { pts[s.slot[p.PlayerID]] = p.PointsX100 }

    // 2. score each DISTINCT signature: ~500k × 11 mul-adds ≈ 5.5M ops ≈ 3 ms
    for sig := range s.sigTable {
        mask, capIdx, vcIdx := decode(sig)
        var total int32
        for slot := range bits(mask) {
            m := int32(100)                       // 1.00×
            if slot == capIdx { m = 200 } else if slot == vcIdx { m = 150 }
            total += pts[slot] * m / 100
        }
        s.sigScore[sig] = total
    }

    // 3. scatter to teams + build histogram in ONE pass: 10M gathers ≈ 40 ms
    var hist [4000]int32
    for i, sig := range s.teamSig {
        sc := s.sigScore[sig]
        s.teamScore[i] = sc
        hist[sc/50]++                             // 0.5-point buckets
    }
    return &Snapshot{Epoch: pp.Epoch, Hist: hist, TopK: s.localTopK()}
}

Compute is not the bottleneck. This is the thing most candidates get wrong. You shard for memory headroom, HA and many concurrent contests — not because the math is hard.

Rebuilding in-memory state after an eviction

A pod holding 10 M teams will get evicted mid-match. Recovery: load the last epoch snapshot from S3, replay the compacted team.roster topic from that offset, replay player.points from that epoch. Cold start under 30 seconds — and it is deterministic, because rosters are frozen at lock so the compacted topic is static for the whole match. Run shards as a StatefulSet with a warm standby consuming the same partitions.

5 · Contest lifecycle — the animated state machine

This is where the design changes character. Press Play and watch the consistency model flip from eventual → provisional → final → money. Click any state to inspect it.

step 1 / 8

Why the provisional window exists. Provider data gets revised — scorecard corrections, DLS adjustments, super overs, retroactive stat fixes, disputed catches. If you pay out the instant the feed says MATCH_COMPLETE, you will eventually pay the wrong person and be unable to claw it back. That "winnings will be credited after review" banner is a deliberate architectural decision, not a UX afterthought.

At FINALIZED, recompute from scratch off the immutable final 40-vector. Never settle from incrementally accumulated in-memory state — you want a computation you can reproduce and defend in an audit six months later.

6 · Winner declaration & settlement

Everything above is disposable. This is the half that has to be right the first time.

Tie handling — pool and split the occupied ranks

Prizes are rank-range based, so ties must pool the prizes for every rank the tied group occupies:

3 teams tie for rank 2 → they occupy ranks 2, 3, 4 → (₹50L + ₹25L + ₹10L) / 3 = ₹28.33L each.

Then round deterministically: after rounding to paise, the residual is assigned by a fixed rule (lowest team_id, or a house rounding account) so that Σ payouts == prize_pool exactly. A leaderboard that is off by ₹0.03 fails reconciliation and blocks the entire settlement run.

Temporal retries. Workers crash. Kafka redelivers. The unique index makes every one of those a no-op. Say this line explicitly in an interview — it is what separates a real answer from a hand-wave.

func SettleContest(ctx workflow.Context, contestID string) error {
    workflow.ExecuteActivity(ctx, FreezeScores, contestID).Get(ctx, nil)

    var ok bool
    workflow.ExecuteActivity(ctx, ReconcileWithOfficialScorecard, contestID).Get(ctx, &ok)
    if !ok {
        // durable block — survives worker restarts, deploys, a weekend
        workflow.GetSignalChannel(ctx, "manual-approval").Receive(ctx, nil)
    }

    var payouts []Payout
    workflow.ExecuteActivity(ctx, ComputePayouts, contestID).Get(ctx, &payouts)

    for _, batch := range chunk(payouts, 500) {
        workflow.ExecuteActivity(ctx, CreditWallets, batch).Get(ctx, nil)
    }
    return workflow.ExecuteActivity(ctx, Reconcile, contestID).Get(ctx, nil)
}

Reconciliation runs after every settlement: Σ payouts == prize pool, count(payouts) == count(winners), wallet deltas match ledger deltas. Alert and freeze on mismatch. Corrections go through maker-checker with a reversing ledger entry — never an UPDATE on a settled row. Abandoned matches reuse the identical machinery in reverse.

8 · Trade-offs worth defending

Why not a Redis ZSET for everything?

ZADD of 10 M members every 20 s is ~500 k ops/s of write churn per contest, plus ~1 GB of skiplist overhead. The histogram gives O(1) rank at 16 KB. Use ZSET only for contests under ~100 k teams where exact "players near me" ordering matters and the cost is negligible — one interface, two implementations selected by contest size.

Why not compute rank per user on read?

Read amplification is the enemy: 10 M users refreshing versus 10 M scores computed once per epoch. Precompute wins by four orders of magnitude. (And why not Spark? Minutes of latency. Wrong tool.)

Why Go/Rust for one service and Java everywhere else?

Exactly one service is latency-and-layout critical. Optimise there, keep velocity everywhere else. Polyglot is a cost — pay it once, deliberately.

Why Temporal and not a Kafka consumer for settlement?

You need durable blocking on human approval, queryable workflow state at 2 AM during a dispute, and retry semantics you didn't hand-roll. A consumer group gives you none of that.

Interview cheat sheet

  1. Clarify: contest sizes (2 → 10 M), freshness (10–30 s is fine), is money involved (yes → the second half changes).
  2. Reduce the problem: "a match is 40 numbers."
  3. Bit-pack + dedup: 8 bytes per team, 10 M → 500 k distinct.
  4. Histogram ranking: O(1) rank, mergeable across shards, 16 KB.
  5. Immutable snapshots + atomic pointer flip: no torn leaderboards.
  6. Monotonic epochs: ranks never go backwards on refresh.
  7. CDN the top-K: it is the same bytes for everyone.
  8. State machine at match end: provisional → review → finalized → settled.
  9. Exactly-once = unique constraint, not delivery semantics.
  10. Reconcile every settlement, and never UPDATE a settled row.
