12FastSpeech 2

An explicit duration predictor and a length regulator replace attention on the decode path: phonemes are repeated into frames, and the whole mel is produced in one parallel pass. The alignment failures of the previous page become unrepresentable. The bill arrives as flattened prosody and a rhythm that is only as good as your forced aligner.

Theory

Attention deleted, not tamed

Tacotron 2 decides how long each phoneme lasts implicitly, as a side effect of when attention moves on. FastSpeech 2 makes that decision explicit and hoists it in front of the decoder. The encoder — four feed-forward Transformer (FFT) blocks, self-attention with a 1-D convolution in place of the position-wise feed-forward layer — turns L phonemes into L hidden vectors. A variance adaptor predicts one duration d_i per phoneme, and a length regulator repeats hidden vector i exactly d_i times. That gives a T = Σd_i frame sequence which four more FFT blocks map to mel in a single parallel pass. No query, no softmax over encoder positions, no step conditioned on the previous step's output. The skipping, repeating and non-terminating babble of the previous page are not mitigated here, they are unrepresentable: a repeat-interleave cannot jump backwards, and T is known before decoding starts.

Where the durations come from

FastSpeech 1 read them off a trained autoregressive teacher's attention matrix and trained the student on the teacher's simplified mel. FastSpeech 2 drops the teacher on both counts: durations come from forced alignment (the paper uses the Montreal Forced Aligner) and the model is trained against ground-truth mel. That is the single biggest quality change between the two versions. Two consequences: your rhythm is only as good as your aligner — the failure modes moved upstream into the labels — and you carry a labelling dependency, not a second model.

The variance adaptor

Three predictors share a shape: a 2-layer 1-D convolution with ReLU, layer norm and dropout, then a linear projection. Order matters. The duration predictor reads the L phoneme hiddens and is regressed in the log domain, where MSE learns the conditional mean of log d — a geometric mean in frames, which pulls the long tail in and is one reason synthetic rhythm sounds tidier than human rhythm. The pitch and energy predictors sit after the length regulator and read the expanded T-frame sequence: pitch trained on a continuous-wavelet decomposition of the F0 contour and inverted by iCWT at inference, energy the L2 norm of each STFT frame's magnitude. Both are quantised to 256 bins, embedded, and added back frame by frame — one control vector per mel frame, not per phoneme, which is what separates this from FastPitch. Training conditions on ground truth, inference on the predictions.

What it buys, and the bill

Compute stops depending on T serially. At 22.05 kHz with a 256-sample hop, one frame is 11.61 ms and a second of audio is 86 frames; an autoregressive decoder pays one forward pass per frame, FastSpeech 2 pays one pass total. FastSpeech 1 reported roughly 270× faster mel generation than Transformer TTS and 38× end to end — their numbers at their batch size, not a guarantee at yours. The bill is the one-to-many mapping: the same text supports many valid prosodies, and a regressor trained with MSE answers with the average of them. Version 2's frame-level pitch and energy conditioning claws some of that back, but the output is still flatter than a good autoregressive model's. That is the honest direction of the trade — non-autoregressive is faster and far more predictable, not more natural.

The characteristic failure

It does not babble. It gets the rhythm wrong. An under-predicted vowel clips and sounds rushed; an over-predicted one drawls. And the sharp edge is rounding: round(d×α) on a short consonant can land on zero, which silently deletes a phone — "sixty" losing its /t/ becomes "sixy". Clamp to one frame minimum, and check yours does before you ship a rate control. Aligner errors show up the same quiet way: a mis-segmented silence inflates one phone's target, and the model learns to drawl in that context forever.

On a call

The reason to want this on telephony is p99, not p50. A fixed T means synthesis time is a deterministic function of text length, so a turn budget of roughly 800 ms has no tail risk from the synthesiser, and batching across concurrent calls is straightforward: every item does identical work. The catch: decoder self-attention is non-causal over the whole frame sequence, so emitting early means chunking text and stitching, not reading frames as they appear. And the variance adaptor's outputs are derived from caller-specific text: log the template ID and the frame count, never the phoneme string or its F0 contour, which inherit the retention rules of the PHI they came from.

Visualization — the length regulator

# Duration predictor: 2-layer 1-D conv (ReLU, LayerNorm, dropout) -> scalar.
# Regressed in the LOG domain against Montreal Forced Aligner targets, so MSE
# learns the conditional MEAN of log d -- a geometric mean in frames.
log_d = duration_predictor(h)                  # h: (L, 256) phoneme hiddens
L_dur = mse(log_d, log(d_mfa + 1))

# Length regulator: repeat, do not attend. alpha < 1 speeds the voice up.
d = round((exp(log_d) - 1) * alpha)
d = clamp(d, min=1)      # <-- round(0.4) == 0 DELETES the phone. clamp it.
frames = repeat_interleave(h, d, dim=0)        # (sum(d), 256) -> frame level

# Pitch and energy predictors come AFTER the length regulator and read the
# EXPANDED sequence; their embeddings are added frame by frame -- one control
# vector per mel frame. (Phoneme-level pitch before expansion is FastPitch.)
f0 = icwt(pitch_predictor(frames))             # CWT target -> T-length F0 contour
en = energy_predictor(frames)                  # L2 norm of |STFT| per frame
frames = frames + pitch_emb[quantise_log(f0, 256)] + energy_emb[quantise(en, 256)]

# 22050 Hz / 256-sample hop -> 11.61 ms per frame, 86.1 frames per audio second.
T   = d.sum()                                  # known BEFORE the decoder runs
sec = T * 256 / 22050                          # 110 frames -> 1.28 s here

The line I'd actually say

"FastSpeech 2 takes the alignment decision out of the decoder. The encoder gives you one hidden vector per phoneme, a duration predictor says how many mel frames each one gets, and the length regulator just repeats them — so the frame count is known before you decode, and the whole mel comes out in one parallel pass instead of one forward pass per frame. Skipping, stuttering and running to max_decoder_steps aren't mitigated, they're unrepresentable, because a repeat-interleave can't move backwards."

"What you pay for it is prosody. It's a one-to-many mapping — lots of valid readings of the same sentence — and duration is regressed with MSE in the log domain, so you get the conditional mean and a flatter rhythm. Version 2's answer is the variance adaptor: pitch and energy predicted after the length regulator and added to the expanded sequence frame by frame, plus training on real mel instead of a teacher's output, and durations from a forced aligner rather than teacher attention. On a phone line I'd take that trade for the p99 — synthesis time becomes a deterministic function of text length, so the synthesiser contributes no tail to an 800 ms turn budget, and batching across calls is trivial because every item does identical work. The two things I'd actually check in code are that durations are clamped to at least one frame, because rounding to zero silently deletes a phone, and that the aligner is good, because its errors are now your rhythm."

Takeaways

  • Length regulator, not attention. repeat_interleave(h, d) expands L phoneme hiddens to T = Σd_i frames, so T is known before decoding and the alignment failures of the previous page cannot occur. In PyTorch that is one line, and it is the whole architectural argument.
  • The variance adaptor is where control and quality both live. Duration is predicted on the phoneme sequence; pitch (CWT-decomposed F0) and energy are predicted after the length regulator, quantised to 256 bins and added to the expanded sequence — one control vector per mel frame. Training uses the ground truth, inference the predictions.
  • Version 2's real change was dropping the teacher. FastSpeech 1 distilled from an autoregressive teacher's attention and its simplified mel; FastSpeech 2 takes durations from Montreal Forced Aligner and trains on ground-truth mel. Your rhythm is now downstream of your aligner.
  • Faster and more predictable, not more natural. MSE on log durations gives the conditional mean, so prosody flattens; that is the price of parallel decode. Get the direction of this trade right — interviewers check it.
  • The failure is rhythm, and at the edge, deletion. round(d×α) hitting zero drops a phone outright; clamp to 1. Export frames-per-phoneme and predicted-vs-aligner MAE to Prometheus so a drifting rate control is visible before a caller reports it.
  • Fixed compute is an ops property. Deterministic T means uniform batches and no synthesiser tail in an 800 ms budget — easy to shape for Triton or an ONNX export — but non-causal decoder self-attention means streaming needs text chunking and stitching, not frame-by-frame emission.
