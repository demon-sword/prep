19Streaming TTS

The caller does not experience your total synthesis time. They experience the gap before your first syllable. Streaming trades that gap against three things you have to pay for: seams at every chunk join, prosody that restarts if you cut the text, and a per-chunk overhead that quietly eats your concurrency.

Theory

What streaming actually buys

Take a real agent turn — “Your appointment with Doctor Ellis is confirmed for Thursday the fourth at ten fifteen, and we'll send a reminder text the day before” — about 7.1 s of speech. On a GPU where acoustic model and vocoder together run at a real-time factor of 0.05, synthesising it in one shot costs 7140 × 0.05 ≈ 357 ms of compute before a single sample exists. Cut at the first phrase boundary and you synthesise 1.66 s: 83 ms. Cut at 320 ms and it is 16 ms. Time-to-first-audio falls by more than an order of magnitude while total compute barely moves.

That matters because of where it lands. Inside an 800 ms budget from the caller's last word to the caller hearing yours, the other stages have already spent most of it: endpoint hangover 200–300 ms, ASR finalisation 50–150, LLM time-to-first-token 200–400, outbound transit plus the far-end jitter buffer 40–80. TTS gets what is left, often under 150 ms. 357 ms does not fit. 16 ms does — same model, different scheduling.

Where you cut the stream

Two genuinely different cut points. Cutting mel frames means the acoustic model has already produced a contiguous frame sequence — an autoregressive decoder emits them left to right anyway — and you call the vocoder on windows of it; prosody is untouched, because the mel was generated as one utterance. Cutting text means separate synthesis calls per clause, which is what you must do while the LLM is still generating and the rest of the sentence does not exist. Non-autoregressive models like FastSpeech 2 can only stream this way: the length regulator and the encoder self-attention need the whole phoneme sequence, so their unit of streaming is the text chunk, not the frame.

When you cut text, keep the first chunk short and let later chunks grow: chunk one is the only one whose synthesis the caller waits on, and every chunk after it has the playback of its predecessor to hide behind.

The seam, and why it clicks

A vocoder generating sample n uses mel frames on both sides of n — HiFi-GAN is fully convolutional and its receptive field spans tens of frames. Hand it a chunk in isolation and both edges are computed against zero padding, context the network never saw in training. The join is a discontinuity: a tick, or a short buzz, once per boundary. At 320 ms chunks that is three per second and it reads as a bad tape edit.

The fix is to synthesise wider than you keep. Carry L mel frames of context on each side into the vocoder call, then discard the samples belonging to those frames and keep the middle. Once L covers half the receptive field the seam is gone; below that it fades rather than disappearing, and a short linear cross-fade over the join covers the residual phase mismatch at the cost of smearing any transient that lands exactly there. Note what the right-hand context costs: it is L frames of audio you wait for, but only L × frame_ms × RTF of wall clock, because the model runs far faster than real time. Two pieces of state get dropped in practice and click the same way — the polyphase resampler's filter history when you go 24 kHz to 8 kHz, and the decoder's hidden state if you restart it per chunk.

Text-chunked streaming cannot fully fix its seams: the next chunk has not been generated, so there is no right-hand context to carry, and the two chunks are independent generations differing slightly in level and phase. You can buy that context by delaying a chunk — which costs exactly what streaming was for.

The cost of small chunks

Context frames are recomputed, so a chunk's real cost is n + 2L frames, not n. Add a fixed per-chunk overhead — kernel launches, host glue, resample, mu-law encode, packetise — and the effective real-time factor is

RTFeff(n) = RTF · (n + 2L)/n + overhead / (n · frame_ms)

Both corrections are O(1/n). At 10.67 ms per frame (24 kHz, hop 256), RTF 0.05, L = 8 and 8 ms of overhead: 50 frames (533 ms) gives RTFeff ≈ 0.081, while 5 frames (53 ms) gives ≈ 0.36. Concurrency per worker is roughly 1/RTFeff, so shrinking the chunk tenfold bought ~25 ms of first-audio and cost three quarters of your capacity — which is why nobody streams at frame granularity. Push further and the stream breaks outright: once RTFeff crosses 1 a chunk takes longer to compute than to play, the playout buffer starves, and the caller hears the agent stutter mid-word.

Failure modes

Three, and one is usually the question. The seam click from insufficient context. The prosody reset, where each text chunk is synthesised as its own utterance, so F0 declination restarts at every boundary and the sentence is delivered as a list of fragments — which is why you cut at clause boundaries, not at a fixed word count. And the underrun stutter above, which is what over-tuning for first-audio actually sounds like.

On a phone line, and with PHI

Your output leg is 20 ms RTP packets — 160 samples of G.711 at 8 kHz. Snap chunks to a multiple of 20 ms or you buffer a partial packet and hand the far-end jitter buffer a ragged arrival pattern. Small chunks are also what makes barge-in cheap: anything already on the wire has been heard, so a 1.5 s chunk in flight is 1.5 s you cannot recall and must keep in the LLM's context. For compliance the record that matters is what was played, not what was generated; a cancelled stream makes those diverge, and the played audio is the PHI. Be honest about the alternative, too: a speech-to-speech model emits audio tokens at a fixed frame rate and has no chunking decision at all. What it gives up is the text seam where you can edit, redact or swap the voice — in a regulated deployment that seam is worth something.

Visualization — the same sentence, batched and streamed

# ---- 24 kHz acoustic model + HiFi-GAN, hop 256 ----------------------------
SR, HOP  = 24000, 256
FRAME_MS = 1000 * HOP / SR         # 10.667 ms of audio per mel frame
RTF      = 0.05                    # measured compute-seconds per audio-second
CTX      = 8                       # mel frames of vocoder context, per side
OVERHEAD = 8.0                     # ms/chunk: launch, resample, mu-law, RTP
NET_MS   = 60.0                    # outbound transit + far-end jitter buffer

def compute_ms(n, sides):          # n = mel frames KEPT from this chunk
    return (n + sides * CTX) * FRAME_MS * RTF + OVERHEAD

def rtf_eff(n, sides):             # both corrections are O(1/n)
    return RTF * (n + sides * CTX) / n + OVERHEAD / (n * FRAME_MS)

# ---- playout schedule: compute is serial, playback is not ------------------
def simulate(chunks, sides):       # chunks = [n_frames, ...]
    done = ttfa = underrun = 0.0
    play_end = None
    for n in chunks:
        done += compute_ms(n, sides)       # worker finishes this chunk
        ready = done + NET_MS              # ...and it reaches the ear
        if play_end is None:
            ttfa = start = ready           # what the caller calls "responsive"
        elif ready > play_end:
            underrun += ready - play_end   # buffer starved: audible gap
            start = ready
        else:
            start = play_end               # seamless: we were ahead
        play_end = start + n * FRAME_MS
    return ttfa, underrun

# batch = one chunk of ~669 frames (7.14 s), no context, no seams
# stream = 320 ms chunks of 30 frames, 2 sides of context: 23 chunks, 22 seams

The line I'd actually say

"Streaming is a scheduling change, not a model change. A seven-second agent turn at RTF 0.05 costs about 350 ms of compute before the first sample exists if you synthesise it in one shot — and by the time TTS starts, endpointing, ASR and the LLM have already spent 600-odd of the 800 ms the caller will tolerate. Cut it into 300 ms chunks and time-to-first-audio drops to a few tens of milliseconds, because you only wait on chunk one."

"The two costs are seams and overhead. HiFi-GAN's receptive field spans tens of mel frames, so a chunk synthesised in isolation has both edges computed against zero padding — that's the click. You fix it by synthesising wider than you keep, carrying context frames on each side and discarding their samples. Waiting for right-hand context is cheap in wall clock, because those future frames cost frame-time times RTF, not frame-time. And the overhead is the reason you don't go smaller: recomputed context plus a fixed per-chunk cost are both order one-over-n, so at a 53 ms chunk — five mel frames — your effective RTF is roughly 0.36 instead of 0.08, and you've lost three quarters of your concurrency. If you cut text rather than frames, add prosody reset — cut at clause boundaries, not word counts, or every fragment gets its own declination contour."

Takeaways

  • Time-to-first-audio is the metric, not total synthesis time. 7.14 s of speech at RTF 0.05 is 357 ms batched and ~16 ms for a 320 ms first chunk. With endpointing, ASR and LLM TTFT already consuming ~650 ms of an 800 ms budget, that difference is the whole call.
  • Know which thing you are cutting. Cutting mel frames leaves prosody intact and gives context on both sides. Cutting text is what you must do while the LLM is still generating, and it costs you right-hand context plus a prosody reset at every boundary — so cut at clauses.
  • The seam is a receptive-field problem. Synthesise wider than you keep: carry L mel frames per side, discard their samples, cross-fade a few milliseconds over the join. Right-hand context costs L × frame_ms × RTF of wall clock, not L × frame_ms. Carry the polyphase resampler state across chunks too, or you re-introduce the click at the 8 kHz downsample.
  • Small chunks are paid for in concurrency. RTF_eff = RTF·(n+2L)/n + overhead/(n·frame_ms) — both terms O(1/n). Track it per deployment: export chunk-level compute from Triton or TorchServe to Prometheus and alarm on RTF_eff, not on mean request latency, which hides underruns entirely.
  • The three failure modes are seam click, prosody reset and underrun stutter. The third is what over-tuning for first-audio sounds like, and it is worse than a later start because it recurs mid-word. Snap chunk sizes to 20 ms RTP packets so the far-end jitter buffer sees a regular arrival pattern.
  • Audio already on the wire has been heard. Large chunks mean a barge-in cannot recall what the caller received, so the LLM context must be truncated to what was played — and it is the played audio, not the generated audio, that is the PHI record you retain. Cache repeated prompts as pre-vocoded Redis blobs keyed by text, and the first chunk gets cheaper still.
