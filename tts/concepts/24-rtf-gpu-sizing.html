24Real-Time Factor and GPU Sizing

“How many GPUs for a thousand concurrent calls?” is a throughput question with a latency answer. Real-time factor gives you the load; queueing theory decides how much of the fleet you are allowed to use; and the way an under-provisioned voice agent breaks is not an error page, it is the agent stopping mid-word.

Theory

RTF, and the convention that flips it

Real-time factor is compute time divided by audio duration: synthesise 10 seconds of speech using 0.3 seconds of GPU time and RTF = 0.03, so below 1 is faster than real time. Half the tooling reports the reciprocal as a speed-up — the HiFi-GAN paper reports its V1 generator at roughly 168× real time on a V100, vocoder only, single stream — so pin down which of the two is on the table, because 0.03 and 33× describe the same machine. Two qualifiers matter as much as the figure. Measure it at your target batch size and under load, not on an idle card — an idle single-stream RTF flatters a fleet that will run dozens of streams per GPU. And carry p95, not the mean: an autoregressive model's cost varies with the utterance, and it is the tail that arrives in a caller's ear as a gap.

Concurrent calls are not concurrent streams

The business gives you calls; the GPU sees audio-seconds per second. In a two-party call the agent speaks for a minority of the wall clock — call it 35% for a triage or scheduling flow, and go and measure it, because it is the largest single lever here. So 800 concurrent calls demand around 800 × 0.35 = 280 audio-seconds of synthesis per second, not 800. Two corrections push back. Barge-in wastes work: audio synthesised, queued and then cancelled when the caller talks over it still cost GPU-seconds, so carry a waste factor of order 10–15%. And ASR is the quiet half of the bill — streaming recognition runs at close to a 100% duty cycle, because you keep listening while the agent talks in order to detect barge-in at all. TTS is the model people size; ASR often dominates the invoice.

The sizing formula

# offered load, in whole-GPU equivalents (Erlangs)
C, duty, waste = 800, 0.35, 0.12   # calls; agent talk share; barge-in waste
rtf1, B, beta  = 0.030, 8, 0.15    # p95 batch-1 RTF (MEASURE); batch; scaling
L              = 0.5               # chunk length, seconds of audio

kappa   = 1 + beta*(B - 1)               # 2.05 - a batch of 8 costs 2.05 singles
rtf_eff = rtf1 * kappa / B                # 0.0077 GPU-s per audio-s
audio   = C * duty * (1 + waste)          # 313.6 audio-seconds per wall second
A       = audio * rtf_eff                 # 2.41 GPU-equivalents of work

# capacity says ceil(A) = 3. The SLO is decided by queueing, not by A.
T = L * rtf1 * kappa                      # 30.8 ms to serve one batch

def erlang_b(c, A):
    b = 1.0
    for n in range(1, c + 1):
        b = (A*b) / (n + A*b)
    return b

def erlang_c(c, A):
    b, rho = erlang_b(c, A), A/c
    return b / (1 - rho*(1 - b))          # P(an arriving batch waits)

# mean queue wait, M/M/c:  erlang_c(c, A) * T / (c - A)
#   c = 3: rho 0.80, wait 35 ms    c = 4: rho 0.60, wait 6 ms
# the last fifth of utilisation costs more latency than the first four

Batching buys throughput with the clock

Model a batch of B as costing κ(B) = 1 + β(B−1) single items, β being the marginal cost of one more: β = 0 is perfect parallelism, β = 1 no benefit at all. Then RTF_eff = RTF₁((1−β)/B + β), which has a floor: as B grows without bound, RTF_eff → β·RTF₁. At β = 0.15 no batch size can ever be more than 6.7× cheaper than batch 1, and B = 40 is already within 15% of it. That is the argument against enormous batches, and why β is measured on your kernel rather than assumed. You pay twice on the clock: a formation wait of roughly (B−1)/2λ while the arrival stream supplies the batch, and a service time κ× longer than a single item. Triton's max_queue_delay_microseconds caps the first: with the window binding, mean formation sits at exactly half the window and the batch fills to 1 + λW rather than to B.

Utilisation is where the SLO dies

Capacity is not a service level. Treat each GPU as a server and Erlang C gives the mean queue wait C(c,A)·T/(c−A) — a hyperbola in c−A. At ρ = 0.7 the wait is a fraction of a service time; at 0.95 it is many. Treat M/M/c as pessimistic, since batch service is nearly deterministic and the standard approximation W(M/G/c) ≈ ((1+cv²)/2)·W(M/M/c) roughly halves it at cv = 0. Then choose utilisation deliberately: 0.7 survives an arrival burst, 0.9 meets its mean and misses its p95, and the gap between them is one GPU on a small fleet.

The failure mode is dead air mid-sentence, not a 503

An overloaded batch API returns errors, so a dashboard tells you. Overloaded streaming TTS returns the correct audio late, and the caller hears the agent stop mid-word for 300 ms and resume. Chunk N+1 has to arrive before chunk N finishes playing, so you survive one chunk length plus your accumulated playout lead, and no longer. The M/M/c waiting-time tail is exact — P(W>t) = C(c,A)·e−(c−A)t/T — so p95 chunk delay is T·ln(C/0.05)/(c−A), going non-linear where ρ does. Alarm on per-chunk deadline misses, not utilisation: 92% and 99% look alike on a utilisation panel and sound completely different on the phone.

What that means for the runbook

Size for the busy-hour peak and hold warm headroom rather than trusting an autoscaler: the knee is seconds wide and a cold replica costs tens of seconds of weight load and graph capture. Re-measure RTF after every export change — a TensorRT or ONNX conversion moves it, and your sizing with it. Two constraints are specific to healthcare voice. A phrase cache is the cheapest capacity you will ever buy, but key it on the fixed template only: a cached utterance carrying a member's name or appointment time is PHI at rest, with a retention obligation attached. And a burst pool must stay inside the regions and accounts your BAA covers, which makes “autoscale into spare capacity” a compliance decision, not an ops one.

The line I'd actually say

“Real-time factor is GPU-seconds per audio-second, and the trap is that it's a throughput number people spend as if it were a latency number. I'd size from offered load: concurrent calls times the agent's talk-time duty cycle — about a third, not one — times a barge-in waste factor, times the batched RTF. Eight hundred calls at 35% with an effective RTF around 0.008 is under three GPU-equivalents of real work, which is usually a surprise to whoever asked.”

“Then I'd refuse to run at that number. Capacity says three; the service level is decided by queueing. Erlang C puts the mean wait at C(c,A)·T over c minus A, so the last twenty points of utilisation cost more latency than the first eighty put together. I'd hold around 0.7 and use batching as the throughput lever, with a hard cap on the queue delay — in Triton that's max_queue_delay_microseconds — so a batch that isn't going to fill doesn't sit there waiting for a ninth request.”

“And the part people miss: an under-provisioned streaming TTS doesn't throw a 503, it returns the right audio late. Chunk N+1 misses the moment chunk N stops playing and the caller hears the agent pause mid-word. So I alarm on per-chunk deadline misses against the playout buffer, not on GPU utilisation — 92% and 99% look nearly the same on a utilisation panel and sound completely different on the phone.”

Takeaways

  • Offered load is one line of arithmetic. A = C · duty · (1+waste) · RTF_eff. 800 calls × 35% agent talk × 1.12 barge-in waste = 313.6 audio-seconds per second; at an effective RTF of 0.0077 that is 2.4 GPU-equivalents. Concurrent calls are not concurrent streams, and the duty cycle is the biggest lever in the expression.
  • Batching has a hard floor. RTF_eff = RTF₁((1−β)/B + β), so it can never beat β·RTF₁ — at β = 0.15 that is 6.7× batch 1, whatever B you choose. You pay in formation wait (B−1)/2λ and a κ× longer service, so cap the window (Triton's max_queue_delay_microseconds) and accept a partly-filled batch.
  • Utilisation, not capacity, sets the SLO. Erlang C wait is C(c,A)·T/(c−A); ceil(A) GPUs runs at ρ near 1 and misses every p95. M/M/c is the pessimistic bound — deterministic service roughly halves it via ((1+cv²)/2) — so size on M/M/c and bank the difference.
  • The failure mode is dead air, not an error. Chunk N+1 must land within one chunk length plus the playout lead. p95 chunk delay is T·ln(C/0.05)/(c−A) from the exact M/M/c tail. Export that deadline-miss counter to Prometheus and put p95, not utilisation, on the Grafana panel.
  • ASR is the quiet half of the bill. Streaming recognition runs near a 100% duty cycle because you listen through the agent's own speech to catch barge-in, against roughly 35% for TTS. Size both, and re-measure RTF after any ONNX or TensorRT export — the conversion moves the number your fleet is derived from.
  • Warm headroom beats autoscaling, and the burst pool is a compliance object. The queueing knee is seconds wide and a cold replica costs tens of seconds of weight load, so Kubernetes HPA arrives after the calls did. Cache fixed prompt templates for free capacity but never the filled slots — a cached utterance with a member name is PHI at rest — and keep burst capacity inside BAA-covered regions.
