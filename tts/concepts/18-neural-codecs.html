18Neural Audio Codecs and RVQ

A language model cannot consume a mel spectrogram — it needs a vocabulary. A neural codec manufactures one: downsample the waveform, quantise each frame with a stack of codebooks that each correct the last one's error, and hand the integers upstream. The stack is the whole trick, and its first stage is the one you cannot repair.

Theory

What the codec is actually for

SoundStream (Zeghidour et al., 2021) and EnCodec (Défossez et al., 2022) share one shape: a fully convolutional encoder that strides the waveform down to a low frame rate, a residual vector quantizer that turns each latent frame into a handful of integers, and a decoder that reconstructs the waveform — trained with a reconstruction loss over multi-scale mel plus adversarial and feature-matching losses from STFT discriminators, the same machinery a vocoder uses. Two different customers want the output. A transport codec wants bitrate. A generative model wants a token stream, because discrete units are what a transformer can be trained to predict.

Memorise the arithmetic; it is what an interviewer probes. EnCodec's 24 kHz model uses encoder strides 2×4×5×8 = 320, so 24,000 samples per second becomes 75 latent frames per second — one frame every 13.3 ms, which is also the streaming model's algorithmic latency. Each 128-dimensional frame is quantised by nq codebooks of 1024 entries: 10 bits per codebook per frame, so 750 bps buys you one codebook. nq = 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 gives 1.5, 3, 6, 12 and 24 kbps. Raw 24 kHz 16-bit PCM is 384 kbps, so 6 kbps is 64:1. SoundStream is the same 320× stride at 24 kHz over 3–18 kbps; both papers report their low-bitrate models rated above Opus at several times the bitrate, which is the claim worth remembering rather than any single MUSHRA number.

Why residual, and not one big codebook

Eight codebooks at 10 bits is 80 bits per frame. A flat quantiser at that budget needs 280 ≈ 1.2×1024 centroids — unstorable, untrainable, and unsearchable. RVQ spends the same 80 bits on 8 × 1024 = 8192 vectors and 8192 distance evaluations per frame. That is the entire justification.

The mechanism is greedy and coarse-to-fine. Set r0 = z. At stage i, pick ci = argmink ||ri-1 − Ci[k]||, then ri = ri-1 − Ci[ci]. Only the residual moves on. Decoding is a sum, not a search. Two consequences follow immediately. First, the stages are not interchangeable: stage 1 removes most of the energy and later stages return sharply diminishing decibels, which is why quality saturates rather than scaling with bitrate. Second, an error in stage 1 is unrecoverable — later codebooks encode the residual relative to the coarse code that was chosen, so a wrong first token is not corrected downstream, it is decorated. In a generative model that is heard as a wrong phoneme or a lurch in speaker identity, not as noise.

Codebooks are trained by exponential-moving-average updates of the cluster centroids plus a commitment loss on the encoder, with dead-code restart: an entry that wins no frames is re-seeded onto a poorly-reconstructed input. SoundStream adds quantizer dropout — a random nq per training example — so one checkpoint serves every bitrate on the nested prefix of its stages.

The token rate is the number that decides your architecture

75 Hz × 8 codebooks = 600 acoustic tokens per second of audio. An autoregressive model that emits all of them serially must decode a token every 1.67 ms to hold real time — before any network leg. That is why nobody does it flat. VALL-E splits the stack: an autoregressive model over the first codebook only (75 tokens/s, a far more forgiving 13.3 ms per token), then a non-autoregressive pass that fills codebooks 2–8 conditioned on it. AudioLM puts semantic tokens from a self-supervised model in front of SoundStream's acoustic tokens and predicts coarse-to-fine. MusicGen flattens several codebooks into one stream with a delay pattern — codebook k offset by k steps — costing nq−1 frames of latency to buy one AR pass. And the newer speech-to-speech codecs attack the frame rate itself: Mimi, the codec inside Moshi, reports 12.5 Hz at roughly 1.1 kbps with 8 codebooks, which pencils out to 11 bits a stage — 12.5 × 8 = 100 tokens/s, a sixth of EnCodec's 600, and the reason a full-duplex model becomes tractable at all.

The phone leg, the failure mode, and the PHI

Do not confuse this with the transport codec. Your RTP leg is G.711 mu-law at 64 kbps or Opus, fixed by the carrier, and a neural codec needs a GPU at both ends — these tokens are an internal representation, not something you put on the wire. What the phone line does do is push the codec off its training distribution: feed a 24 kHz model 8 kHz mu-law audio upsampled to 24 kHz and the top two thirds of the band are empty, a region no training frame ever occupied. Reconstruction goes watery and sibilants smear.

The characteristic failure is codebook collapse. A large fraction of entries win no frames, so a nominal 10 bits per stage carries far fewer — usage entropy, not codebook size, is your real bitrate. It presents as quality plateauing while nq rises: you pay 750 bps for a stage that removes almost no residual. Watch per-stage code perplexity in training, not just the loss. Finally, acoustic tokens reconstruct an identifiable speaker: a cached token sequence is a recording, so a token cache or prompt log in a healthcare deployment inherits the same consent, retention and disclosure obligations as the WAV file, and a codec fine-tuned on caller audio is a PHI-derived artefact.

Visualization — watch the residual come down, stage by stage

# ---- EnCodec 24 kHz: where every number on this page comes from ------------
sr, strides, K, n_q = 24000, [2, 4, 5, 8], 1024, 8
stride = 2 * 4 * 5 * 8                 # 320 samples per latent frame
fps    = sr / stride                   # 75.0 frames/s -> 13.3 ms per frame
bits   = math.log2(K)                  # 10 bits per codebook per frame
kbps   = fps * n_q * bits / 1000       # 75 * 8 * 10 = 6000 bps = 6 kbps
tok_s  = fps * n_q                     # 600 acoustic tokens per second

# ---- RVQ: greedy, one stage corrects the last one's error ------------------
def rvq_encode(z, books):              # z: (T, 128) encoder output
    r, codes = z.copy(), []
    for C in books:                    # C: (K, 128), one stage
        k = ((r[:, None, :] - C[None]) ** 2).sum(-1).argmin(-1)
        codes.append(k)
        r = r - C[k]                   # only the residual moves on
    return np.stack(codes, 1), r       # (T, n_q); r = what nothing captured

def rvq_decode(codes, books):          # decode is a sum, not a search
    return sum(C[codes[:, i]] for i, C in enumerate(books))

The line I'd actually say

"A neural codec is how you get a vocabulary out of audio. EnCodec's 24 kHz model strides the waveform down by 320, so 75 latent frames a second, and each frame goes through a stack of 1024-entry codebooks where each stage quantises what the previous stage got wrong. Ten bits a stage at 75 Hz is 750 bps per codebook, so eight codebooks is 6 kbps. It's residual rather than flat because eighty bits flat would be 280 centroids — residual buys the same budget for eight thousand vectors."

"For a voice agent the number I actually care about isn't the bitrate, it's the token rate: 75 times 8 is 600 tokens a second, which is 1.67 ms per token if you decode them serially. So you don't — VALL-E runs autoregressive over the first codebook only, 13.3 ms a token, and fills the rest non-autoregressively. That's also where the failure mode lives: stage one is unrecoverable, because later codebooks only encode the residual around the coarse code you already picked. A wrong first token doesn't sound like noise, it sounds like the wrong phoneme. And I'd watch code-usage entropy in training — collapsed codebooks mean you're paying ten bits a stage to carry four."

Takeaways

  • The frame-rate arithmetic is the whole interview. Strides 2×4×5×8 = 320 at 24 kHz gives 75 Hz and 13.3 ms of algorithmic latency; 1024 entries is 10 bits, so 750 bps per codebook and nq = 8 is 6 kbps, 64:1 against 384 kbps PCM. Derive it live rather than quoting it.
  • Residual, because flat is impossible. 80 bits per frame means 280 centroids flat versus 8×1024 = 8192 vectors for RVQ. The cost is that the stages are ordered and stage 1 dominates — quality saturates with nq, it does not scale.
  • Token rate, not bitrate, sizes the model. 600 tokens/s is 1.67 ms per serial decode. VALL-E's AR-over-q=0 plus a NAR pass, MusicGen's delay pattern, and Mimi's 12.5 Hz frame rate are three different answers to the same constraint.
  • Codebook collapse is the failure mode. Usage entropy, not log₂K, is your effective bitrate. EMA updates, commitment loss and dead-code restart keep entries alive; quantizer dropout makes one PyTorch checkpoint serve every bitrate on a nested prefix of stages.
  • Codec tokens are not a transport codec. The PSTN leg stays G.711 or Opus; a 24 kHz codec fed upsampled 8 kHz mu-law is off-distribution and goes watery. Serve encoder and decoder as separate Triton models, export via ONNX, and alarm per-stage code perplexity in Prometheus.
  • Tokens are PHI. They reconstruct an identifiable speaker, so a token cache in Redis or a prompt log is a recording — same consent, retention and disclosure rules as the audio, and a caller-fine-tuned codec is a PHI-derived artefact.
