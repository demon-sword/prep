28Watermarking, Consent and Voice Safety

What you owe once the voice is synthetic: a mark that survives a phone line, a consent record you can revoke, a disclosure the model cannot talk its way out of, and a waveform that is PHI however clean the transcript.

Theory

A watermark is a claim about your output, not about the world

An audio watermark is a low-level signal added to synthesised speech that a matched detector can recover and a listener cannot hear. Modern ones — AudioSeal, SynthID, WavMark — are learned: a generator emits a perturbation conditioned on the host audio, and a detector trained against simulated distortions scores every frame. The payload is tiny, tens of bits, so it identifies a model version or a tenant, not a sentence. And detection is statistical: evidence accumulates over the window, so the statistic grows as roughly the square root of window length — a 150 ms clip is a far weaker claim than two seconds. The honest framing: a watermark makes your own output attributable. It says nothing about audio from a model that does not watermark, so absence of a mark is not evidence of a human. Provenance tool, not deepfake detector.

The phone line is the attack chain, and it is not adversarial

Before an adversary attacks your watermark, the network does. A 24 kHz render is resampled to 8 kHz, band-limited to roughly 300–3400 Hz, companded to 8-bit mu-law and packetised — and some never arrive. If the mark's energy sits above 4 kHz, where it is easiest to hide from a listener, resampling deletes it outright and no detector recovers it. So on a PSTN leg the mark must live in the same narrow band as the speech it hides in, exactly where hiding is hardest. Mu-law is comparatively mild: a roughly constant ~38 dB signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio across its dynamic range. Codecs that resynthesise rather than waveform-code are worse than their bitrate suggests. Packet loss hurts twice: it shortens the effective window, and concealment fills the gap with unwatermarked audio.

Consent is a data structure, not a checkbox

Consent for a cloned voice has scope, a term and a revocation path; revocation is the engineering problem. You cannot cheaply un-train a base model, so keep the speaker droppable: a conditioning embedding or a per-speaker adapter, so withdrawal is deleting an artifact and re-serving rather than retraining. Provenance is the matching corpus requirement: every training utterance carries its source, consent record, licence and date, or “was this voice in the model” has no answer. Zero-shot cloning inverts the threat model: the reference clip is now an input, so a system that clones from arbitrary audio clones the caller off a recording. Restrict that path to enrolled voices.

Disclosure, and the model that will lie about it

Assume you must tell the caller the voice is synthetic and the call recorded. The regimes differ — the FCC's February 2024 ruling brought AI-generated voices in robocalls under the TCPA's artificial-voice restrictions, California's B.O.T. Act requires bot disclosure in some commercial contexts, and the EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency duties apply from August 2026 — none of it legal advice, but the engineering answer is stable. Play a pre-rendered disclosure: nothing in the per-turn budget, because it is not synthesised in the loop. Then hard-code the answer to “am I talking to a robot?” as a deterministic branch ahead of the LLM. A prompt instruction is not a control; a model told to be reassuring will deny it.

In healthcare the voice is itself the identifier

HIPAA's Safe Harbor list names biometric identifiers including voice prints, so call audio does not stop being PHI when you redact the transcript — the waveform still carries an identifier the redaction never touched. Retention windows apply to the audio, not only the text; any vendor whose GPU touches it needs a BAA and a written no-training answer; and deletion must fan out to everything derived, because a speaker embedding from patient audio is a biometric identifier too, as are ASR caches and prompt logs. The cheapest mitigation is not holding the waveform at all — transcribe, scrub, drop — decided at the first pipeline stage, not in a quarterly review.

Where the milliseconds land

Watermark embedding is a small convolutional pass over audio you already have; at an illustrative real-time factor of 0.05 it adds about 10 ms to a 200 ms streaming chunk. That lands on time-to-first-audio inside an 800 ms turn budget, so measure it rather than assuming it is free. Detection is the opposite: run it offline over stored calls, never in the turn loop.

The line I'd actually say

“Watermarking makes my own output attributable — it doesn't detect deepfakes, because absence of a mark just means it came from a model that doesn't watermark. And on a phone call the network is already the attack: 24 kHz down to 8, band-limited to roughly 300–3400 Hz, mu-law, packet loss. Hide the mark at 6 kHz where it's inaudible and resampling deletes it, so it has to sit in the narrow band — the hardest place to hide it. And detection is a correlation over a window, going as the square root of duration, so a 150 ms barged-in chunk is near undetectable.”

“On consent I'd care most about revocation, because you can't un-train a base model — keep the speaker as an adapter or embedding so withdrawal deletes an artifact instead of triggering a retrain, and carry a consent record on every training utterance. And never let arbitrary reference audio into the cloning path, or you'll clone the caller off their own recording.”

“For healthcare the thing people miss is that the waveform is the identifier — voice prints are on HIPAA's Safe Harbor list — so scrubbing the transcript doesn't de-identify the audio at all. Retention applies to the waveform, deletion has to reach the embeddings and ASR caches, and any vendor touching it needs a BAA. Disclosure I'd play as pre-rendered audio at call start, free in the turn budget, and hard-code 'am I talking to a robot' ahead of the LLM — a prompt instruction isn't a control.”

Takeaways

  • A watermark is provenance, not detection. It shows audio came from your system; it cannot show audio came from a person. Say that before anyone proposes it as anti-fraud.
  • Put the mark where the phone line will not delete it. A PSTN leg keeps roughly 300–3400 Hz, so an inaudible high-band mark is gone after resampling. Mu-law's ~38 dB quantisation noise is the mild part; resynthesising codecs and storage re-encoding are worse.
  • Detection needs duration. d′ grows as √window and shrinks with payload bits, so barge-in truncation and packet loss attack the detector directly. Detect offline; embedding still costs ~10 ms per 200 ms chunk on time-to-first-audio.
  • Design for revocation up front. Per-speaker adapters make withdrawal a delete; a fine-tuned monolith makes it a retrain. Keep utterance-level provenance in a versioned Parquet manifest, lineage in MLflow.
  • The waveform is PHI even when the transcript is clean. Voice prints are Safe Harbor identifiers, so retention and deletion cover audio, embeddings, ASR caches and prompt logs alike. Run the fan-out as an Airflow DAG; emit consent and disclosure events to an append-only Kafka topic.
  • Disclosure belongs in code, not the prompt. Pre-rendered notice at call start, deterministic branch for “are you a bot”. A model asked to be reassuring will deny being a model.
