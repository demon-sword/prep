20Latency Budget Decomposition

The caller does not experience your p50 inference time. They experience one number: the silence between their last word and your first syllable. Every stage in the pipeline spends part of it, most teams measure each stage separately and never measure the sum, and the largest single line item is usually the one nobody thinks of as a model at all.

Theory

Define the clock before you argue about it

Start the stopwatch when the last speech sample leaves the caller's mouth and stop it when your first sample reaches their ear. That definition is deliberately harsher than the one most dashboards use, because it includes two things engineers habitually exclude: the inbound network leg, which has already elapsed before your ASR sees a byte, and the outbound leg plus the far-end jitter buffer, which elapses after your metrics have declared the request complete. Both are inside the silence the caller is sitting in.

For calibration: in ordinary human conversation the gap between turns is startlingly short — Stivers et al. (PNAS, 2009) found the modal transition across ten languages sits near zero, with language means spread over a few hundred milliseconds. Separately, ITU-T G.114 puts acceptable one-way mouth-to-ear transmission delay at 150 ms, degrading through 400 ms. Note those are different quantities and they add: G.114's budget is the two network legs below, and your agent's thinking time sits on top of it. The 800 ms figure used throughout this page is a design convention, not a measured constant — it is roughly where callers stop reading the pause as thought and start reading it as a broken line.

The eight line items

Walk a tuned cascade end to end. Inbound leg: 20 ms of packetisation, 25 ms of transit, 30 ms of jitter buffer — 75 ms, and you cannot optimise it because it is already spent. Endpoint hangover: the silence your VAD waits through before declaring the turn over, 320 ms. ASR finalisation: flushing the decoder, rescoring, punctuating — 120 ms. LLM prefill: 180 ms for a couple of thousand tokens of system prompt and history. Decode to the first clause: this is the one people get wrong — TTS cannot start on a token, it needs a synthesisable span, so about 10 tokens at 18 ms each, 180 ms. Guardrail: the PHI redaction or policy filter that must inspect text before it is spoken, 40 ms. TTS time-to-first-audio: 60 ms for a streamed first chunk. Outbound leg: 85 ms. Summed serially that is 1060 ms, and the call feels broken.

What actually overlaps

The hangover window is the only free overlap resource in the pipeline, and it is large. During those 320 ms you already have the audio; you are merely withholding the decision that the turn ended. A streaming ASR can finish decoding inside it, so finalisation costs max(0, F − H) rather than F. Speculatively, you can prefill the LLM on the last stable partial transcript in the same window — different hardware, so both can consume the window concurrently — making prefill cost max(0, P − H). With F and P both under 320 ms, 300 ms of the budget disappears and the same pipeline lands at 760 ms. The bill for speculation is wasted compute whenever the caller resumes talking and the prefill must be discarded, which on a mid-sentence pause is often.

Four things genuinely cannot be hidden. Both network legs are physics. The endpoint decision is a decision about the future, so nothing downstream of it can commit early. And the guardrail is serial by construction: its whole purpose is to sit between generation and the wire.

Budget the tail, not the mean

A p50 budget is a budget for a call nobody is on. Two effects push the tail out. First, queueing: a GPU stage at utilisation ρ has, under the optimistic M/M/1 assumption, sojourn time W = S/(1−ρ) — at ρ = 0.8 a 180 ms prefill becomes 900 ms, and your budget is gone to a stage whose service time never changed. Second, per-stage variance adds. Working in favour: for independent stages the p95 of the sum is below the sum of the p95s, because standard deviations add in quadrature — means grow like n, spread like √n. So budgeting each stage's p95 separately and summing them is conservative, but a normal approximation of the total is optimistic, because real latency distributions are right-skewed.

The failure mode

Dead air has a signature that is worse than the pause itself. Past roughly a second the caller says “hello?”, which arrives as speech while your response is finally starting — the barge-in detector cancels the in-flight synthesis, the LLM is re-invoked with the new turn, and the next response is later still. The system does not degrade gracefully; it oscillates, and the transcript afterwards reads as an agent that keeps abandoning its own sentences. The measurement failure is the sibling of it: each team reports its own stage's p50, every dashboard is green, and no one owns the sum. Instrument one span from the last-speech-frame timestamp to the first-RTP-write timestamp, with a call-level correlation ID, and alarm on its p95.

The phone line, PHI, and the honest S2S comparison

Latency debugging in healthcare is a place people quietly leak PHI: it is tempting to dump call audio to reproduce a slow turn, and you do not need to. Timestamps and correlation IDs reconstruct the budget completely; audio adds nothing and turns a metrics store into a regulated one. A speech-to-speech model collapses ASR finalisation, prefill, decode-to-clause and TTS into a single audio-in-to-audio-out time-to-first-token, which on the numbers above can be several hundred milliseconds cheaper. It does not remove the network legs, and if it does its own turn-taking it moves the hangover inside the model rather than deleting it. What it removes that you may want is the text seam — the guardrail stage above has nowhere to run when there is no text between the model and the wire. That is the real trade, and it is not settled.

The line I'd actually say

“I'd define the clock first, because most people measure the wrong thing: last speech sample out of the caller's mouth to first sample in their ear, which means both network legs and the far-end jitter buffer are inside the budget even though they never show up in our request traces. On a typical cascade that's eight stages — inbound leg, endpoint hangover, ASR finalisation, LLM prefill, decode to the first clause boundary, guardrail, TTS first-audio, outbound leg. Laid end to end that's about a second, and roughly 800 ms is where a caller stops hearing thought and starts hearing a broken line.”

“The interesting part is that the biggest line item isn't a model — it's the endpoint hangover, and it's also the only free overlap resource you have. During those 300 ms you already have the audio, you're just withholding the decision, so streaming ASR can finalise inside it and you can speculatively prefill the LLM on the last stable partial. That's three hundred milliseconds for free, at the cost of throwing prefill away when the caller resumes. Then I'd budget the p95, not the p50, because a GPU at 80% utilisation multiplies its queued stages by five — S over one minus rho — without the model getting any slower.”

Takeaways

  • The budget starts at the caller's last speech sample and ends at their eardrum. Both network legs and the far-end jitter buffer are inside it. ITU-T G.114's 150 ms one-way transmission target covers only those legs — your agent's thinking time sits on top of it, not inside it.
  • The hangover is usually the largest line item and the only overlap resource. ASR finalisation costs max(0, F−H) and speculative prefill max(0, P−H), so a 1060 ms serial cascade lands at 760 ms with no model change. The bill is discarded prefill when the caller resumes mid-pause.
  • TTS starts on a clause, not a token. Budgeting only LLM time-to-first-token hides ten more tokens of decode — ~180 ms at 18 ms/token — between TTFT and the first synthesisable span.
  • Budget the tail. An M/M/1 stage at ρ = 0.8 has W = S/(1−ρ) = 5× its service time with no model regression. Independent per-stage variances add in quadrature, so summing per-stage p95s is conservative while a normal approximation of the total is optimistic.
  • Instrument one span, not eight dashboards. Emit last-speech-frame to first-RTP-write with a call correlation ID, export from Triton or your gateway to Prometheus, alarm on p95 in Grafana, and record ρ alongside it — on Kubernetes the same latency regression appears whenever the HPA lets replicas saturate. Cache system-prompt prefixes and repeated-phrase audio in Redis; a KV-cache hit is the cheapest 200 ms you will ever find.
  • Dead air oscillates rather than degrading. Past ~1.2 s the caller says “hello?”, that speech cancels your in-flight synthesis, the turn restarts, and the next response is later still. Speech-to-speech collapses four stages into one TTFT but removes the text seam your PHI guardrail runs in — that trade is live, not settled.
