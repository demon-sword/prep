31Serving Cost Economics for TTS

What a minute of synthesised speech actually costs once you stop counting GPU-seconds and start counting rented hours — batching, phrase caching, the engineering carry nobody puts in the spreadsheet, and where the line crosses per-character vendor pricing.

Theory

What a synthesised second costs

Start from the only unit the card understands. Real-time factor is GPU-seconds per audio-second, so the marginal cost of a second of speech is rtf_eff × price_per_gpu_second. At an illustrative $1.20 per GPU-hour that is $0.000333 a GPU-second; with a measured p95 batch-1 RTF of 0.05 served at batch 8 the effective figure is 0.0128 GPU-seconds per audio-second, so an audio-second costs $4.3 × 10−6 and an audio-minute about $0.00026. Per-character vendor pricing — take an illustrative $15 per million characters, against roughly 840 characters in a minute of speech at 14 chars/s — is $0.0126 a minute, and it does not move with volume. Marginal compute is some fifty times cheaper, and that comparison is close to worthless, because you do not buy GPU-seconds. You rent whole GPUs by the hour, busy or idle.

You provision for peak and pay for the wall clock

The fleet is sized by peak concurrent synthesis, not by monthly volume. Half a million calls a month at four minutes with the agent speaking 35 % of the time, 12 % of that synthesised and then discarded to barge-in, and 30 % served from a phrase cache, is 548,800 synthesised minutes — 32.9 million audio-seconds, which averaged over a 730-hour month is only 12.5 audio-seconds of synthesis per wall-second. But clinic traffic is not spread over 730 hours. It lands in roughly nine hours a day, five days a week, with an intraday peak on top, so peak concurrency runs on the order of five times the month mean: about 63 audio-seconds per wall-second, 1.15 GPU-equivalents at 70 % target utilisation, which ceil rounds to two and an N+1 spare makes three. Three GPUs at $1.20 for 730 hours is $2,628 a month, or $0.0048 per synthesised minute — nineteen times the marginal figure, and the fleet is busy about 5 % of the hours you pay for. It still beats $0.0126, by about 2.6×. The idle share is the product: real-time voice cannot queue until morning.

The two lines the spreadsheet leaves out

The first is engineering carry. Self-hosting means a serving stack, a voice-quality regression suite, model upgrades that change how the agent sounds, capacity work and someone on call at 3 a.m. when a clinic's inbound line is silent. Half an engineer fully loaded — call it $9,000 a month, illustratively — is 3.4× the GPU bill at three cards, and it moves the build-versus-buy crossover from about 127,000 calls a month to about 904,000 — a factor of seven, decided entirely by the line item that never makes it into the RTF arithmetic. The second is compliance. The text you submit to a vendor is PHI — “your results from Dr Chen are ready” is a diagnosis-adjacent disclosure — so a vendor needs a signed BAA, contractual zero retention, and no training on your submissions. If you cannot get all three, this stops being an economics question and the crossover is irrelevant.

Caching, and why it favours the vendor

Voice agents repeat themselves. The recording disclosure, the synthetic-voice disclosure, hold prompts, menu options and the fixed carrier phrases around slot-filled confirmations are the same audio every call. Key a cache on normalised text plus voice ID plus model version, and a 30 % hit rate is unremarkable; the hits cost a Redis lookup rather than a GPU. Two cautions. A rendered confirmation naming a patient and an appointment is PHI as audio, so the cache lives inside the boundary, encrypted, with a TTL and no public CDN in front of it — cache the invariant carrier phrases and synthesise the slots. And a cache scales the vendor bill linearly while it scales yours in whole GPUs, so near the crossover a good cache moves the decision toward the vendor; far above it, both bills fall by the same factor and the ratio is unchanged.

Batching and the integer wall

A batch of B costs κ = 1 + β(B−1) singles, so rtf_eff = rtf1 κ/B. With β = 0.15, batch 8 costs 2.05 singles for 8 streams and batch 32 costs 5.65 for 32 — 0.0128 against 0.0088 GPU-seconds per audio-second, 31 % less compute. Whether that is money depends on where ceil falls. At the volume above it takes the fleet from three GPUs to two, worth $876 a month; at eleven GPUs it would save three, and at three hundred it saves about ninety. Below about ten cards, an RTF win only pays when it crosses an integer boundary. It is also not free: batch service time is L × rtf1 × κ, so on 0.5-second chunks batch 8 takes 51 ms and batch 32 takes 141 ms. You are trading roughly 90 ms of every caller's turn, inside an 800 ms budget, for $876 a month. At three GPUs that is a bad trade; at three hundred it is an obvious one.

The characteristic failure

Costing the pilot at the marginal rate. You quote $0.00026 a minute off the RTF, and the first invoice is twenty times that, because you bought always-on capacity for a nine-hour clinic day. The mirror-image failure is winning the price argument and losing the SLO: the cheapest fleet is the one running at 95 % utilisation, which is the one where p95 time-to-first-audio disappears into queueing. Size on peak, price on the wall clock, and keep the utilisation and the p95 on the same Grafana board so nobody optimises one blind to the other. And note that TTS is usually the cheap leg: streaming ASR runs at close to a 100 % duty cycle because you keep listening while the agent talks, and the LLM is billed per token on every turn.

The model this panel computes

# Cost per minute of SYNTHESISED speech: self-hosted vs per-character vendor.
# Every price is illustrative. Substitute your quote and your MEASURED p95 RTF.
V, L, duty     = 500_000, 4.0, 0.35   # calls/month; minutes; agent talk share
waste, hit     = 0.12, 0.30           # barge-in discard; phrase-cache hit rate
rtf1, B, beta  = 0.050, 8, 0.15       # p95 batch-1 RTF; batch; batch scaling
gpu_hr, carry  = 1.20, 9_000          # $/GPU-hour; engineering carry $/month
per_1M, cps    = 15.00, 14.0          # vendor $ per 1M chars; chars/s of speech
peak_mean, u   = 5.0, 0.70            # peak:month-mean concurrency; util ceiling
HOURS          = 730                  # billable hours in a month

synth_min = V * L * duty * (1 - hit) * (1 + waste)     # 548,800 min/month
synth_s   = synth_min * 60                             # 32,928,000 audio-s

rtf_eff = rtf1 * (1 + beta*(B - 1)) / B                # 0.01281 GPU-s / audio-s
mean_as = synth_s / (HOURS * 3600)                     # 12.53 audio-s per wall-s
gpus    = ceil(mean_as * peak_mean * rtf_eff / u) + 1  # ceil(1.15) = 2, +1 spare
infra   = gpus * HOURS * gpu_hr                        # 3 * 730 * 1.20 = $2,628
util    = synth_s * rtf_eff / (gpus * HOURS * 3600)    # 0.054 -- you rent 730 h,
                                                       # the clinic is open ~200
self_pm   = (infra + carry) / synth_min                # $0.02119 all-in
vendor_pm = cps * 60 * per_1M / 1e6                    # $0.01260 -- volume-free

# crossover = smallest volume at which the fixed legs amortise below vendor_pm
#   GPUs only, $1,752 floor  ->  ~127,000 calls/month
#   plus $9,000/mo of carry  ->  ~904,000 calls/month  (the fleet's step
#                                 from 3 to 4 cards pushes it back above once)

The line I'd actually say

“I'd start from RTF, but I wouldn't stop there, because the marginal number is misleading. At batch 8 with a p95 batch-1 RTF of 0.05 you're spending about 0.013 GPU-seconds per audio-second, so a minute of speech is a couple of hundredths of a cent of compute against maybe a cent and a quarter of per-character vendor pricing. Fifty to one. But you don't buy GPU-seconds — you size on peak concurrency and pay by the hour, and clinic traffic lands in about nine hours a day, so peak runs five-ish times the month mean. At half a million calls a month that's three cards including an N+1 spare, running at roughly five percent utilisation, and the real figure is about half a cent a minute. Still under the vendor, by about two and a half times.”

“The thing that actually decides it is the line nobody puts in the sheet: the serving stack, the voice regression suite, the upgrades and the on-call. Half an engineer is several times the GPU bill at three cards, and it moves the crossover from something like a hundred and thirty thousand calls a month up to the better part of a million. And before any of that — the text we submit is PHI, so if the vendor won't sign a BAA with contractual zero retention and no training on our submissions, the crossover doesn't matter, we self-host. Two things I'd watch after launch: utilisation and p95 time-to-first-audio on the same board, because the cheapest fleet is the one that's queueing, and batch size, because past batch 8 you're buying compute with milliseconds of every caller's turn.”

Takeaways

  • Marginal compute is not your cost. rtf_eff × $/GPU-second gives roughly $0.00026 per audio-minute at batch 8 and $1.20/hour — but you rent whole cards for 730 hours to serve a nine-hour clinic day, so the rented-hardware figure is around $0.0048 — $0.0212 once carry is in — and the fleet is busy ~5 % of what you pay for. Size on peak, price on the wall clock.
  • The carry decides build-versus-buy, not the RTF. Serving stack, regression suite, upgrades and on-call — illustratively half an engineer at $9,000/month — moved the crossover from ~127,000 to ~904,000 calls/month at the defaults. The curve first dips under the vendor line at ~841,000, but the fleet's step from three cards to four pushes it back above, so ~904,000 is where it stays below. Put it in the spreadsheet or the spreadsheet is fiction.
  • Batching pays only when it crosses an integer. κ = 1 + β(B−1), so batch 8→32 cuts compute 31 % but adds ~90 ms of batch service time. Below ten cards ceil eats the win; use Triton dynamic batching with a window bounded by your TTFA budget, not by throughput.
  • Cache the carrier phrases, synthesise the slots. Disclosures, menus and hold prompts hit a Redis lookup instead of a GPU — but rendered audio naming a patient is PHI, so keep it encrypted, TTL'd and inside the boundary, never behind a public CDN. A cache shifts the vendor bill linearly and yours in whole GPUs, so near the crossover it argues for buying.
  • PHI can settle it before economics do. The text you submit is PHI; a vendor needs a signed BAA, contractual zero retention and no training on submissions. Without all three the crossover is moot.
  • Watch cost and latency on one board. The cheapest fleet is the one at 95 % utilisation, which is the one where p95 TTFA becomes queueing. Export utilisation and TTFA to Prometheus, chart them together in Grafana, and let Kubernetes scale on concurrent streams with headroom for model load time.
