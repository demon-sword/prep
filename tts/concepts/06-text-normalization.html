06Text Normalization for Speech

The unglamorous front end that decides whether "$1.5M on 3/4/26" is read correctly — number and date expansion, abbreviation handling, and homograph disambiguation where "read", "live" and "lead" need part-of-speech context the acoustic model never sees.

Theory

The stage in front of everything

A TTS model reads phonemes, so something must turn Take 1-2 tablets q4h, DOB 3/4/26 into words before grapheme-to-phoneme runs. That is text normalization: written to spoken form, serial in front of everything else, and the one stage whose errors are silently plausible. A failing vocoder buzzes. A failing normalizer says "one minus two tablets" in a natural voice, and the caller acts on it.

Classify, then verbalize

The standard framing (Sproat et al., 2001) sorts non-standard words into semiotic classes — cardinal, money, date, time, telephone, measure, electronic, letter sequence, verbatim — each with its own verbalizer. Verbalizing is the easy half and wants to be deterministic: a compiled weighted finite-state transducer (Kestrel, its open-source sibling Sparrowhawk, NVIDIA NeMo's WFST grammars) is auditable and cannot invent a number. Choosing the class is the hard half: the evidence is not in the token. 3/4/26 is a US date, a UK date, or a fraction. St. is saint or street. MRN 004821 must be read digit by digit, leading zeros included, because "four thousand eight hundred twenty-one" is a different patient.

Homographs are the same ambiguity inside ordinary words: "read", "live" and "lead" each have two pronunciations selected by part of speech, and CMUdict holds both — READ R IY1 D and READ(1) R EH1 D. A POS tagger picks; wrong, and the acoustic model cannot recover. 

# Classify the semiotic class, then verbalize per class. The classifier
# holds the ambiguity; the verbalizers are deterministic.
DATE = re.compile(r'^(\d{1,2})/(\d{1,2})/(\d{2,4})$')

def classify(tok, prev):
    if DATE.match(tok):        return 'DATE'
    if prev.upper() in ID_WORDS or tok.startswith('0'): return 'DIGITS'
    if tok.isdigit():          return 'CARDINAL'
    return 'WORD'

def verbalize(tok, cls):
    if cls == 'DIGITS':   return ' '.join(DIGIT[c] for c in tok)  # keeps 004821
    if cls == 'CARDINAL': return n2w(int(tok))                    # drops the zeros

Rules, neural, and streaming

A neural seq2seq normalizer covers more of the tail but fails differently: Sproat and Jaitly (2016) found RNN normalizers produce rare but unrecoverable errors — a quantity read as a different quantity — which a grammar cannot do. The compromise is a learned classifier feeding deterministic verbalizers, routing low-confidence tokens to a safe reading.

Streaming sharpens it. LLM tokens arrive one at a time, so $1.5 cannot be normalized until M lands: verbalizers need lookahead to a safe boundary, which is why streaming TTS chunks at clause boundaries. The compute is small; the position is not. In front of a ~150 ms time-to-first-audio target, a 30 ms tagger spends a fifth of the budget before one mel frame exists — measure yours. And normalizer debug logs hold the member ID and date of birth in expanded form: they are PHI logs.

The line I'd actually say

"Normalization is a classify-then-verbalize problem, and almost all the difficulty is in the classifier. Once I know 004821 is a medical record number rather than a cardinal, verbalizing it is a lookup table; deciding that needs context the token doesn't carry. So I'd keep the verbalizers as finite-state grammars — WFST, Sparrowhawk or NeMo-style — because they're auditable and structurally can't invent a different number, and put whatever learning I need into the class decision and the POS tag that picks between read and read."

"The reason I wouldn't hand the whole thing to a seq2seq model is the failure mode. Sproat and Jaitly showed neural normalizers make rare but unrecoverable errors — a quantity read as a different quantity — and in a healthcare call that's a dosing error delivered in a confident voice with no exception raised anywhere. And it's on the critical path: it's serial in front of G2P and a roughly 150 ms time-to-first-audio, and under streaming you need lookahead to a clause boundary before you can safely verbalize, because $1.5 isn't done until the M arrives."

Takeaways

  • Two stages, and only one of them is hard. Classify the token into a semiotic class — cardinal, date, money, measure, telephone, digits, verbatim — then verbalize with a per-class grammar. Verbalizing is a deterministic table; classifying needs context the token does not carry.
  • Keep the verbalizers finite-state, put the learning in the classifier. A compiled WFST cannot hallucinate a different number; Sproat and Jaitly (2016) showed neural seq2seq normalizers make rare unrecoverable errors. Ship the classifier as a small model — trained in PyTorch, exported to ONNX so it runs in-process on the media host rather than as another network hop.
  • The failure mode is silent plausibility. Nothing throws. "004821" becomes "four thousand eight hundred twenty-one", "1-2 tablets" becomes "one minus two", "q4h" gets pronounced as a word — each in a fluent voice the caller believes. Alert on classifier confidence and unknown-token rate, not on exceptions; those are the counters worth exporting to Prometheus.
  • It is serial in front of the whole pipeline. Nothing can be phonemized until it is verbalized, so the cost lands entirely in time-to-first-audio — a 30 ms tagger is a fifth of a 150 ms TTFA target. Cache verbalizations of the fixed phrases an IVR repeats (a Redis lookup keyed on the raw string) and the recurring cost goes to near zero.
  • Streaming needs lookahead. You cannot normalize "$1.5" before "M" arrives, or "3/4" before you know whether a year follows, so buffer LLM output to a clause boundary before verbalizing — the same boundary streaming TTS chunks on.
  • Normalized text is still PHI. It carries the member ID, the date of birth and the diagnosis in expanded form. Debug logs of normalizer input and output are protected records under the same retention and access rules as the recorded audio — hash or redact before they leave the box.
