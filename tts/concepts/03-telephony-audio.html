03Telephony Audio Constraints

Why a voice that sounds excellent in the demo degrades on an actual phone call: 8 kHz narrowband truncating every fricative, G.711 mu-law quantisation, and a 24 kHz TTS voice losing exactly the band that makes s, f and th tell each other apart.

Theory

The passband is tighter than Nyquist

A PSTN leg carries 8 kHz, 8-bit companded samples — G.711, 64 kbit/s each direction. Nyquist puts a hard ceiling at 4 kHz, but the network is tighter than its own sample rate: the reference channel passband is roughly 300–3400 Hz (ITU-T G.712). So you lose two things at once, the bottom two octaves and everything above 3.4 kHz. A male talker's F0 sits around 85–180 Hz and a female's around 165–255 Hz, which means the fundamental itself is frequently below the passband and simply never transmitted. Intonation survives anyway, because the ear infers pitch from harmonic spacing rather than from the fundamental's presence — the missing-fundamental effect. Phone voices sound thin, not atonal.

What the top of the band actually costs

Above 3.4 kHz is where the sibilants live. /s/ concentrates its energy roughly 4–10 kHz, higher for female and child talkers; /ʃ/ ("sh") peaks lower, around 2–4 kHz, so it partly survives; /f/ and /θ/ are diffuse and weak, spread thinly across a wide band with little to distinguish them below 3 kHz. The result is the classic telephone confusion set — f, th and s collapse into each other — which is exactly why humans spell names over the phone as "S as in Sierra". This is an information-theoretic loss, not a modelling one: the cue is not in the signal, so no downstream ASR recovers it. In a healthcare intake flow the payload most at risk is precisely the high-value part — surnames, member IDs, drug names — and the mitigation is dialogue design (explicit confirmation, phonetic alphabet, DTMF fallback), not a bigger acoustic model.

mu-law is a compander, not a quantiser

G.711 does not quantise amplitude uniformly. It applies a logarithmic compression first, then a uniform 8-bit quantiser to the compressed value, so the effective step size grows with level. The consequence is that signal-to-quantisation-noise stays roughly flat — about 38 dB — across a wide range of input levels, instead of collapsing on quiet speech the way linear 8-bit PCM does. Eight companded bits buy you roughly the dynamic range of 13–14 linear bits. mu-law is the North American and Japanese law; A-law is the same idea with a different curve, used in Europe. Its characteristic artifact is a low-level graininess and idle-channel hiss, not the harsh crunch of a linear 8-bit truncation.

The failure you cause yourself: the resample

Your synthesiser emits 24 kHz. The leg needs 8 kHz. That is decimation by 3, and it is only correct if you low-pass below 4 kHz first. Skip the filter and a 6 kHz sibilant component folds to |6000 − 8000| = 2000 Hz — straight into the vowel space, audible as a metallic whistle riding on every /s/. This artifact gets worse as your vocoder gets better, because a better vocoder puts more real energy up there to fold down. Order matters: filter, decimate, band-limit, compand.

Where it lands in the budget

A 20 ms G.711 packet is 160 samples = 160 bytes of payload; add 12 B RTP, 8 B UDP and 20 B IPv4 and you send 200 bytes 50 times a second — 80 kbit/s on the wire for a 64 kbit/s payload. Opus at 24 kbit/s cuts that payload by about 2.7×, gives you wideband if both ends negotiate it, and costs 26.5 ms of algorithmic delay by default (20 ms frame + 6.5 ms lookahead) against zero for G.711, which is memoryless. Both pay the same 20 ms of packetisation, so the incremental cost of choosing Opus is the 6.5 ms lookahead — call it 7 ms of an 800 ms turn budget to halve bandwidth, usually the right trade. Codec choice is a small term next to endpointing hangover and LLM time-to-first-token. G.114 asks for ≤150 ms one-way mouth-to-ear, and a tandem chain — Opus on the SIP leg, transcoded to G.711 on the PSTN leg — pays both codecs' impairment and both packetisation delays.

Train on what you serve. A model trained on clean 16 kHz and served on a mu-law leg is in a train/serve mismatch you can measure. The standard fix is augmentation: round-trip your training audio through an actual G.711 encode/decode and a low-bitrate Opus encode/decode, and evaluate WER per codec rather than in aggregate. And the recording you keep to do that is voice — in a healthcare setting it is PHI, subject to retention limits and a BAA, whether it sits in object storage or in a feature store. 
# 24 kHz TTS output -> PSTN leg. The order of these three steps is the whole point.
import numpy as np, scipy.signal as sps

def to_pstn(x24, fs_in=24000, fs_out=8000, mu=255):
    # 1. Anti-alias BEFORE decimating. Skip this and 6 kHz folds to
    #    |6000 - 8000| = 2000 Hz, right into the vowel space.
    x8 = sps.resample_poly(x24, up=1, down=fs_in // fs_out)   # 3:1, FIR built in

    # 2. The network's own passband, ~300-3400 Hz (ITU-T G.712).
    sos = sps.butter(4, [300, 3400], btype='band', fs=fs_out, output='sos')
    x8  = sps.sosfilt(sos, x8)

    # 3. mu-law: log compander, THEN a uniform 8-bit quantiser. Step size
    #    grows with |x|, so SNR stays ~flat (~38 dB) with level. Real G.711
    #    uses a 15-segment piecewise-linear approximation of this curve.
    c = np.sign(x8) * np.log1p(mu * np.abs(x8)) / np.log1p(mu)   # -> [-1, 1]
    return np.round((c + 1) / 2 * 255).astype(np.uint8)          # 8 bits @ 8 kHz

"Stripped" means the passband carries under 40% of that phone's energy. For a fricative that is the identifying cue gone — /f/, /θ/ and /s/ have nothing left to tell them apart. For a nasal it mostly means quieter: /m/ and /n/ lose the murmur below 300 Hz, but their identity also lives in the formant transitions into the neighbouring vowel, which the passband still carries.

The line I'd actually say

"The number that matters isn't 8 kHz, it's 3.4 kHz — the PSTN passband is about 300 to 3400 Hz, tighter than its own Nyquist. That band holds vowels and /ʃ/ fine, but /s/ lives up around 4 to 10 kHz and /f/ and /θ/ are diffuse and weak, so on a phone line those three collapse into one another. That's an information loss, not a model deficiency — which is why I'd solve surname and member-ID capture with confirmation prompts, a phonetic alphabet or DTMF, not with a better ASR."

"Then the two things I'd actually check in the code. One, the resample is filtered before it's decimated — 24 kHz down to 8 is 3:1, and if you just take every third sample a 6 kHz sibilant folds to 2 kHz and whistles on every /s/, and it gets worse the better your vocoder is. Two, that we train on what we serve: round-trip the training audio through a real G.711 encode and a low-rate Opus encode and report WER per codec. On latency the codec is a small term — Opus costs 26.5 ms of algorithmic delay by default against zero for G.711 — but both pay the same 20 ms of packetisation, so the incremental cost is really the 6.5 ms lookahead. Call it 7 ms of an 800 ms turn budget. The real money is in endpointing hangover and time-to-first-token."

Takeaways

  • 3.4 kHz, not 4 kHz. The PSTN passband is roughly 300–3400 Hz (ITU-T G.712), tighter than the 4 kHz Nyquist of its own 8 kHz sampling. You lose the fundamental at the bottom — pitch survives via harmonic spacing, the missing-fundamental effect — and the sibilant region at the top.
  • f / th / s is the confusion set, and it is not fixable in the model. /s/ concentrates energy around 4–10 kHz, /ʃ/ around 2–4 kHz so it partly survives, /f/ and /θ/ are diffuse and weak. Once the band is gone the cue is gone; handle it with confirmation prompts, a phonetic alphabet or DTMF.
  • Filter, then decimate, then compand. 24 kHz → 8 kHz is 3:1; without an anti-alias low-pass a 6 kHz component lands at |6000−8000| = 2000 Hz as a whistle on every /s/. Use resample_poly or torchaudio's resampler rather than slicing the array — and note the artifact grows as the vocoder improves.
  • mu-law buys flat SNR, not more bits. Log companding then uniform 8-bit quantisation holds signal-to-quantisation-noise near 38 dB across a wide level range, worth about 13–14 linear bits. A-law is the European equivalent. In the demo, compare the loud-phone and quiet-phone SNR pair: mu-law barely moves across the level spread, linear PCM at the same bit depth falls away.
  • Codec latency is a small term; count it anyway. Opus defaults to 26.5 ms algorithmic delay (20 ms frame + 6.5 ms lookahead) versus roughly zero for G.711, and 20 ms G.711 packets cost 80 kbit/s on the wire for 64 kbit/s of payload. G.114 wants ≤150 ms one-way; tandem Opus↔G.711 transcoding pays both impairments.
  • Train on what you serve, and treat the corpus as PHI. Augment with real G.711 and low-rate Opus round trips in the PyTorch data pipeline, and break WER out per codec in Prometheus rather than reporting one aggregate — a narrowband regression hides inside a wideband average. The recordings you keep to do this are regulated voice data.
