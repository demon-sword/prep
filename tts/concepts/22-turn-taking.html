22Turn-Taking and Endpointing Policy

Deciding the caller has finished is the single largest tunable line in a voice agent's response budget, and the only one you can change without touching a model. A fixed silence timeout is fast and wrong on anything dictated; a semantic endpointer is right more often and has a floor it cannot beat.

Theory

What the endpointer decides, and what the VAD does not

A VAD answers a per-frame question: is there speech in these 20 ms? The endpointer answers a conversational one: does this run of non-speech frames mean the floor is mine? Get the VAD wrong and you mislabel a frame; get the endpointing policy wrong and you talk over a patient reading an insurance number off a card.

The threshold is a line item, not a setting

Endpoint silence is serial dead time: it starts when the caller stops and nothing downstream can begin until it expires. A typical cascade on a phone leg, with a 700 ms fixed threshold: 

# serial path, caller's last sample -> first agent audio in their ear
endpoint_wait   = 700   # the knob
asr_finalise    = 120   # rescore + emit final
llm_ttft        = 320
tts_ttfa        = 150
net_in + net_out = 140  # two legs + jitter buffers
total           = 1430  # ms

# adaptive: threshold as a function of utterance completeness p
wait(p) = clamp(t_min + (t_max - t_min) * (1 - p), t_min, t_max)
# t_min 200, t_max 1600 -> "my member ID is" p=.05 waits 1530 ms,
# "yes, Tuesday works" p=.95 waits 270 ms

Human turn transitions run on the order of a couple of hundred milliseconds, so 1430 ms is a pause the caller notices — and the 700 is nearly half of it. Cut it to 350 and you are at 1080 without retraining anything, which is why people cut it and how agents start interrupting.

Fixed timeout versus semantic endpointing

The fixed policy fires after N milliseconds of contiguous non-speech. It is correct for confirmations and wrong for dictation: a caller reading a member ID off a card produces 600–900 ms mid-utterance gaps as they look at the next chunk, and a 700 ms timer fires inside the number. Raise it to 1200 and every yes/no answer costs an extra half second.

Semantic endpointing asks a different question: is what I have so far a complete utterance? Score the running ASR partial with a small classifier — a fine-tuned encoder over the partial text, exported to ONNX and run on the media node — and make the threshold a function of that score. The result is a quarter-second yield on a sentence that looks finished — faster than any safe fixed value — and about a second and a half of grace on one that does not.

It is not free. The classifier cannot score a word the ASR has not emitted, so partial latency (80–200 ms) plus inference sets a floor the adaptive policy cannot beat, and at high completeness that floor is your threshold. It reads text, so ASR errors propagate: a dropped final word makes a finished sentence look unfinished and the agent waits the full cap. And a hard maximum is mandatory — a caller who trails off with “so…” keeps the score low forever, and without the t_max clamp the agent never speaks again.

The asymmetry that sets the tuning

The two errors are not equal. Dead air costs a beat and a “Hello?” — which then gets endpointed, so the agent answers the hello instead of the request. Cutting the caller off destroys information: they restart, the ASR now holds a fragment plus a repeat, and the agent replies to half a sentence. Tune asymmetrically — short thresholds only where completeness is high.

What the phone line does to this

Two things bite. Carrier-side silence suppression: with VAD and comfort noise (RFC 3389) the far end stops sending during silence and emits sparse SID frames, so “silence” arrives as absent packets. An endpoint timer driven only by decoded frames stalls and never fires — treat a packet gap as silence, not as loss. Second, the jitter buffer sits in front of everything: 60 ms of depth means your silence clock starts 60 ms late — threshold you have already spent.

PHI, and the honest speech-to-speech trade

Partial transcripts are PHI. A semantic endpointer implemented as a hosted LLM call ships every partial off-site — a disclosure needing a BAA, and a network round trip inside the fastest loop in the system. Run it locally. Full-duplex speech-to-speech models learn turn-taking from the acoustics directly, so they see the falling pitch and the hesitation that the cascade throws away at the ASR boundary. What you give up is the knob: when it interrupts a caller there is no threshold to raise and no log line saying which policy fired. Genuinely unsettled.

The line I'd actually say

“Endpointing is the biggest single number in my response budget and the only one I can move without touching a model — a 700 ms silence threshold, plus 120 for ASR finalisation, 320 for time-to-first-token, 150 for first audio and about 140 of network, is 1.4 seconds before the caller hears anything. So the temptation is to cut the 700, and that's where agents start talking over people.”

“The reason a fixed timeout can't win is that mid-utterance pauses aren't shorter than end-of-utterance pauses — someone reading a member ID off a card leaves 800 ms between chunks. So I'd score the running ASR partial for completeness and make the threshold a function of it: about 270 ms when the utterance looks finished, up to about a second and a half when it clearly isn't, with a hard cap so a caller who trails off doesn't stall the agent forever.”

“Two caveats I'd raise unprompted. It can't beat the ASR partial lag plus inference, so at high completeness that floor is the threshold — and the partials are PHI, so the classifier runs on the media node, not as a hosted LLM call. And I'd tune asymmetrically: dead air costs a beat, cutting someone off costs the turn and the data in it.”

Takeaways

  • The endpoint threshold is serial dead time. 700 + 120 + 320 + 150 + 140 ≈ 1430 ms to first audio. It is nearly half the budget and the cheapest line to cut — which is exactly why it gets cut too far.
  • Mid-utterance pauses are not shorter than final ones. A caller reading an ID leaves 600–900 ms between chunks, so no fixed timeout separates “thinking” from “finished”. Only the content of the partial does.
  • Make the wait a function of completeness, with a hard cap. wait(p) = t_min + (t_max − t_min)(1 − p), clamped. Without the cap, a caller who trails off keeps p low and the agent never takes the floor.
  • Semantic endpointing has a floor it cannot beat. ASR partial latency plus classifier inference; at high p that floor is your threshold. Run the classifier as ONNX on the media node — partials are PHI, and a hop to Triton or a hosted LLM adds a round trip inside the tightest loop you own.
  • Instrument the disagreement, not the average. Export cut-off rate, endpoint-to-first-audio and policy-disagreement counts to Prometheus, alarm on p95 in Grafana, and keep per-call turn state in Redis so a cut-off and the caller's restart reconcile into one turn.
  • Tune asymmetrically. Dead air costs a beat and a “Hello?” that itself gets endpointed; a cut-off destroys the turn and its content. Short thresholds only where completeness is high.
