04Jitter, Packet Loss and Concealment

What the network does to audio between your synthesiser and the caller's ear — jitter buffers trading latency for smoothness, packet loss concealment interpolating the gaps, and why a TTS stream that dumps large chunks into the socket damages both.

Theory

The receiver has to un-shuffle a stream that left perfectly paced

Voice over RTP goes out on a metronome: 20 ms of audio per packet, 50 packets a second — 160 samples of G.711 behind a 12-byte header carrying a sequence number and a media timestamp. The network does not preserve that spacing: queues at every hop add a variable delay, so arrivals clump and occasionally reorder. Jitter is the variation in that transit delay, and RFC 3550 fixes the estimator every stack reports: compute D = (Ri − Rj) − (Si − Sj), the change in relative transit time against the previous packet received, then smooth it as J += (|D| − J)/16. That 1/16 gain is a ~16-packet time constant, so J tracks sustained congestion and ignores one stray packet.

A decoder needs 20 ms of audio every 20 ms, forever, and cannot consume a clumped stream. The de-jitter buffer converts one into the other: hold arrivals, order by sequence number, release each at a fixed playout deadline t₀ + n·20 ms. A packet arriving after its deadline is discarded — downstream, indistinguishable from a network drop. So a jitter buffer does not reduce loss; it buys a lower effective loss rate with latency.

The exchange rate, with numbers

Model the excess delay above the path minimum as exponential with mean σ. Crude, but the tail shape is right and it gives a closed form: late-discard rate for a buffer of depth B is exp(−B/σ). Every additional σ divides late loss by e, so 1% down to 0.1% costs σ·ln 10 ≈ 2.3σ — at σ = 12 ms, 28 ms more. Buffers hold whole packets, so round up to a multiple of 20. And you pay it twice: the caller's inbound leg carries one buffer and your outbound TTS leg carries another, so 60 ms each side is 120 ms of an 800 ms turn budget. G.114 wants ≤150 ms one-way mouth-to-ear in total, which is what stops you buffering the problem away.

Loss is bursty, and that is the whole problem

Independent-loss arithmetic badly under-predicts what a caller hears. At 1% uniform loss and 50 pps, runs of two start at 50·(0.01)² = 0.005/s — one 40 ms hole every 200 seconds. Real loss fits a Gilbert–Elliott two-state model far better: a good state that rarely drops and a bad state that drops heavily, with — illustratively — a mean burst length of 3. At the same 1% average that is 0.5 lost packets per second in bursts of three — one 60 ms hole every six seconds. Identical headline metric, completely different call. A loss percentage without a burst-length distribution beside it is close to useless.

Concealment fills the holes. G.711 Appendix I estimates the pitch period from recent history, repeats it into the gap and overlap-adds at the seam, attenuating over successive 10 ms steps and muting by around 60 ms — repeating a pitch period longer than that is an obvious buzz. So the concealment budget is one to three packets. Past that you need redundancy: Opus in-band FEC embeds a coarse copy of the previous frame in the current packet, recovering an isolated loss but forcing the decoder to wait for packet n+1 before giving up on n — another 20 ms of buffer. RFC 2198 RED does the same more bluntly, costing bandwidth. Opus 1.5 added a neural PLC and DRED, a compressed redundant history that stretches recoverable gaps well past waveform repetition, at decoder cost.

The failure mode, and what a big TTS chunk does to it

The characteristic failure is underrun: the buffer is empty at the deadline, PLC expands, and the caller hears a metallic stretch of the last vowel then comfort noise. Adaptive buffers such as WebRTC's NetEq refill by time-stretching later audio without changing pitch, so a real underrun sounds like a stutter-and-catch-up. That adaptivity is what an unpaced sender poisons: write a 1.5-second TTS chunk to the socket as fast as the NIC accepts it and the receiver sees 75 packets in ~60 ms then nothing for 1.4 s. The RFC 3550 estimate explodes, the adaptive buffer grows to absorb variation the path never had, and the burst tail-drops at the first bottleneck queue into exactly the long correlated gap concealment cannot cover. Pace at 20 ms; chunk the synthesis, not the transmission.

The pcap is PHI. You debug all of this with a packet capture, and an RTP capture of a healthcare call is recorded patient voice. SRTP on the wire; strip payloads when the question is timing; full-payload captures inherit the call recording's retention clock and BAA — never a ticket attachment, never an engineer's laptop. 
# The two decisions a de-jitter buffer makes, and how deep to make it.
import math

PTIME = 20        # ms of audio per RTP packet (50 packets/s)

class Jitter:
    """RFC 3550 interarrival jitter. R = arrival clock, S = RTP timestamp,
    both in ms. The 1/16 gain is a ~16-packet time constant."""
    def __init__(self): self.J, self.prev = 0.0, None

    def update(self, R, S):
        if self.prev is not None:
            pR, pS = self.prev
            D = (R - pR) - (S - pS)         # relative transit-time change
            self.J += (abs(D) - self.J) / 16
        self.prev = (R, S)
        return self.J

def playout_deadline(seq, t0, B):
    # Fixed cadence. Arrive after this and you are discarded as late --
    # downstream that is indistinguishable from a network drop.
    return t0 + seq * PTIME + B

def size_buffer(sigma_ms, target_late=0.001):
    # Excess delay ~ Exp(mean=sigma)  =>  P(late) = exp(-B/sigma).
    # Each extra sigma divides late loss by e; 1% -> 0.1% costs 2.3*sigma.
    B = -sigma_ms * math.log(target_late)
    return math.ceil(B / PTIME) * PTIME     # buffers hold whole packets

# sigma = 12 ms, 0.1% late target -> 82.9 ms -> 100 ms of buffer, per leg.
# Two legs = 200 ms of an 800 ms turn budget. Usually too expensive; take 1%.

The line I'd actually say

"A jitter buffer doesn't remove loss — it converts latency into a lower effective loss rate, because anything arriving after its playout deadline is discarded and looks exactly like a network drop. If I model the excess delay as an exponential with mean σ, late loss is e−B/σ, so each extra σ of buffer divides it by e, and 1% down to 0.1% costs about 2.3σ. At σ = 12 ms that's another 28 ms — and I pay it on both legs, so it's real money against an 800 ms turn budget with G.114 wanting 150 ms one-way total."

"The number I'd actually ask for is burst length, not loss percentage. At 1% uniform loss you get a double-packet hole every couple of minutes; at 1% with a Gilbert–Elliott mean burst of three you get a 60 ms hole every six seconds, and G.711 concealment gives up around 60 ms because pitch repetition past that just buzzes. And the thing I'd check in our own code first: that we pace RTP at 20 ms instead of writing a whole 1.5-second TTS chunk to the socket at once — an unpaced burst tail-drops at the bottleneck and simultaneously inflates the far end's measured jitter, so the adaptive buffer grows to absorb variation we caused ourselves."

Takeaways

  • The buffer is an exchange rate, not a fix. Late packets are discarded and are indistinguishable from lost ones. With an exponential delay tail of mean σ, late loss is exp(−B/σ): each extra σ divides it by e, 1%→0.1% costs 2.3σ, and you round up to whole 20 ms packets. Charged on both legs against G.114's 150 ms one-way.
  • Ask for burst length, not loss percentage. 1% uniform loss at 50 pps gives a 40 ms double-gap every ~200 s; 1% with a Gilbert–Elliott mean burst of 3 gives a 60 ms gap every ~6 s. You cannot get this from RTCP receiver reports: an RFC 3550 RR block carries fraction lost, cumulative lost, extended highest sequence number, J, LSR and DLSR — the loss fields are interval averages, so no series of RRs reconstructs burst structure. RTCP XR VoIP-metrics (RFC 3611) adds burst/gap density and duration, but those are averages too. For an actual gap-length histogram, instrument your own depacketiser: count runs of consecutive missing sequence numbers and export them as a Prometheus histogram and put that panel in Grafana beside the loss counter, because the average alone hides the call people complain about.
  • Concealment runs out at roughly 60 ms. G.711 Appendix I repeats the estimated pitch period with progressive attenuation and mutes by about 60 ms. Beyond one to three packets you need redundancy: Opus in-band FEC (costs one packet of extra buffer, ~20 ms), RFC 2198 RED (costs bandwidth), or Opus 1.5's neural PLC and DRED (costs decoder compute — a PyTorch-trained model shipped inside the codec).
  • Underrun is the failure mode, and it stutters rather than gaps. An empty buffer at the deadline makes the PLC expand; NetEq-style adaptive buffers then time-stretch subsequent audio to refill, so it sounds like a metallic stretch and catch-up. If you only alert on packet loss you will never see it.
  • Pace the RTP, chunk the synthesis. Writing a 1.5 s TTS chunk to the socket back-to-back sends 75 packets in ~60 ms: the burst tail-drops at the first bottleneck queue into one long correlated gap, and it inflates the far end's RFC 3550 J, so an adaptive buffer adds latency to absorb variation you created.
  • The packet capture is PHI. SRTP on the wire; strip payloads when the question is timing; if you keep full-payload captures they inherit the call recording's retention clock and BAA. This is the one debugging artefact that quietly leaves the compliance boundary.
