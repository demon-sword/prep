Post-Training: Why & How

Why pretrained models need post-training and the three main solutions: SFT, preference finetuning (RLHF/DPO), and Best-of-N.

Theory

The Pretrained Model Problem

Pretraining produces a powerful statistical engine — a model that has internalized the distribution of text from trillions of tokens scraped from the internet, books, and code. But raw pretraining has a deeply inconvenient property: the model learned to continue text, not to follow instructions, answer questions helpfully, or refuse harmful requests. If you ask GPT-3 "What is the capital of France?" it might respond with ten more geography quiz questions, because that's what naturally follows the pattern of quiz text in its training distribution.

There are three structural gaps a pretrained base model exhibits:

  • Instruction-following gap: The model predicts likely continuations, not intended responses. It cannot distinguish "user turn" from "assistant turn" without post-training.
  • Factual reliability gap: Pretraining doesn't penalize confidently wrong answers. The model learns to sound fluent, not to be accurate.
  • Safety and alignment gap: Web-scraped data contains toxic content, misinformation, and harmful instructions. The base model has absorbed all of it and has no filter.

Post-training is the family of techniques that patch these three gaps without throwing away the expensive pretraining knowledge.

Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

SFT is the first and most fundamental post-training step. You collect a dataset of (instruction, ideal response) pairs — typically 10K–500K examples — and train the model via standard cross-entropy loss on the response tokens only (the instruction tokens are masked from the loss). The model learns the conversational format: "when I see a user request structured like this, I produce a response structured like that."

SFT data quality dominates quantity. OpenAI's InstructGPT paper (2022) demonstrated that 13K high-quality human-written demonstrations outperformed 100K lower-quality examples. Modern SFT datasets use a mix of: human-written gold examples (expensive, high quality), teacher model distillation (a frontier model or Claude generating responses — controversial because of license restrictions), and synthetic generation with filtering.

SFT teaches format and style extremely efficiently but is limited in teaching quality discrimination. It optimizes to match the demonstration, not to maximize human preference. If the demonstration is mediocre, the model learns mediocrity. This is why SFT alone is insufficient — you need the next stages.

Stage 2: Preference Fine-Tuning — RLHF and DPO

Preference fine-tuning teaches the model to produce outputs that humans prefer over alternatives. The core insight is that it's much easier for humans to say "output A is better than output B" than to write a perfect output from scratch. This comparative signal is richer than SFT's imitation signal.

RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) takes a three-stage approach: (1) train a reward model on preference pairs (chosen vs rejected), (2) use PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) to optimize the SFT model's policy against the reward model, while keeping a KL-divergence penalty to prevent the policy from deviating too far from the original SFT model. The KL term is critical — without it, the model learns to game the reward model (reward hacking) by producing outputs that score high but are actually bad.

DPO (Direct Preference Optimization, 2023) eliminates the reward model entirely. It reformulates the RLHF objective such that the optimal policy can be expressed directly in terms of the reference SFT model and the preference pairs — no RL loop required. DPO loss: -log σ(β·log(π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)) - β·log(π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x))), where y_w is the chosen (winner) response and y_l is the rejected (loser). DPO is far simpler to implement and often matches RLHF quality, which is why it's now the dominant choice.

Stage 3: Best-of-N (Inference-Time Compute)

Best-of-N is technically not a training method — it's an inference strategy. Generate N independent completions, score each with a reward model, return the highest-scoring one. This is the simplest form of inference-time compute scaling. With N=32 and a decent reward model, you can achieve performance comparable to a significantly larger model. The cost is linear in N (you pay N forward passes), but the quality improvement is logarithmic — going from N=1 to N=4 helps a lot, N=4 to N=64 helps less, N=64 to N=256 helps even less.

Best-of-N is often used as a baseline to evaluate reward model quality: a good reward model should produce a monotonically increasing quality curve as N grows. If it plateaus or decreases, your reward model is overfit or miscalibrated.

The Post-Training Stack in Practice

Modern frontier models (a frontier model, Claude, Gemini) use all three stages in sequence: SFT → RLHF (or DPO) → constitutional AI / RLAIF refinement. Open-source stacks like a modern open-weight model Instruct use SFT + DPO. Smaller commercial deployments often skip RLHF and use SFT + DPO alone because RLHF requires significant engineering infrastructure (reward model serving, PPO training stability, human labeler pipelines).

Production Reality: Post-training typically requires 1–10% of pretraining compute but can recover 80–90% of the alignment gap. The cost of human preference data (labeler time, quality control) is often the real bottleneck — not compute. A good preference dataset with 50K pairs can cost $50K–$500K depending on quality requirements.

Key Takeaways

  • SFT teaches format, preference tuning teaches quality: SFT alone produces instruction-following but not quality discrimination. You need preference data (RLHF or DPO) to teach the model to prefer better responses over plausible but worse ones.
  • DPO has essentially replaced RLHF for most teams: DPO achieves comparable alignment without a reward model or RL loop. a modern open-weight model Instruct, Mistral Instruct, and most open-source aligned models use SFT + DPO. Only frontier labs with massive labeler pipelines still invest in full RLHF.
  • Reward hacking is the main RLHF failure mode: Without a KL-divergence penalty, PPO optimization causes the model to exploit the reward model — producing verbose, sycophantic, or stylistically unusual outputs that score high but are actually poor. The KL coefficient (β) is the most important RLHF hyperparameter.
  • SFT data quality beats quantity by 10-100x: InstructGPT showed 13K curated examples outperform 100K noisy ones. LIMA (2023) showed 1,000 carefully selected SFT examples produce surprisingly strong instruction-following. Invest in data quality pipelines, not raw annotation volume.
  • Best-of-N is a powerful evaluation tool: A Best-of-N curve (quality vs log N) reveals reward model calibration. If the curve is non-monotonic or plateaus at N=4, your reward model is overfit. Production deployments at Anthropic and Google use Best-of-N with N=4–32 for high-stakes generation tasks where latency allows.
  • Constitutional AI / RLAIF reduces human labeler dependency: Anthropic's RLAIF uses Claude to generate preference labels instead of humans, reducing cost by 10-100x with modest quality trade-off. This is increasingly standard — pure human labeling at scale is economically unsustainable.
