GPU Profiling with Nsight

The two-tool workflow that finds where GPU time really goes — Nsight Systems for the system-wide timeline of kernels, copies, and stalls, then Nsight Compute for the per-kernel roofline that tells you exactly which fix to apply.

Theory

Two Tools, Two Questions

GPU profiling answers two questions in strict order, and each question owns one tool. Nsight Systems (nsys) answers where is time going: it records a system-wide timeline — CUDA kernel launches, host-to-device and device-to-host copies, NCCL collectives, CPU thread activity, dataloader waits, even OS runtime events — on one shared time axis. Nsight Compute (ncu) answers why is this kernel slow: it replays a single kernel and reports SM throughput, DRAM throughput, achieved occupancy, cache hit rates, and a roofline plot placing the kernel on the compute-bound vs memory-bound spectrum. Running ncu before nsys is the classic beginner error: you get a perfect diagnosis of a kernel that accounts for 3% of runtime.

nsys — the timeline

System-wide: kernels, memcpy, NCCL, CPU, dataloader. Low overhead. Always first. Finds gaps and stalls.

ncu — the microscope

One kernel at a time: roofline, occupancy, memory charts, SASS-level hotspots. High overhead (replay). Always second.

The rule

nsys finds the top kernels and the gaps between them; ncu explains the top kernel. Never optimize what nsys has not indicted.

Reading an nsys Timeline: Gaps Are the Finding

A healthy inference timeline looks like a solid wall of back-to-back kernel blocks on the GPU row. Real timelines have four recurring pathologies. Launch gaps — whitespace between tiny kernels — mean the CPU cannot feed the GPU fast enough; small-batch decode with hundreds of microsecond-scale launches is the textbook case, fixed with CUDA graphs or kernel fusion. Memcpy in the hot loop — H2D blocks interleaved with compute — means weights or activations shuttle needlessly; the fixes are pinned memory, batching transfers, and overlapping copies on a separate stream. Dataloader stalls — long GPU-idle stretches while CPU rows show data loading — are a host-side bug wearing a GPU costume. Exposed NCCL — collective blocks with no compute overlapping them in tensor-parallel serving — means communication is fully on the critical path; overlapping communication with computation (or reducing TP degree) is the lever.

SM utilization below ~70% on a serving trace with a non-trivial batch size is the trigger to dig: either the batch is too small to fill the chip, or one of the four pathologies above is fragmenting the timeline.

Reading an ncu Report: The Roofline Decides the Fix

Nsight Compute's SpeedOfLight section reduces a kernel to two percentages: achieved SM (compute) throughput and achieved DRAM throughput, each against the chip's roof. High DRAM with low SM means memory-bound: optimize access patterns (coalescing, tiling, layout) or reduce bytes (lower precision, KV-cache quantization). High SM with low DRAM means compute-bound: raise arithmetic intensity or pick a faster algorithm (tensor-core layouts, FlashAttention, fused kernels). Both low means latency-bound: occupancy, divergence, or launch overhead — check the Occupancy and Launch Statistics sections before touching the math.

The Memory Workload Analysis section then tells you which memory level is biting: L1/TEX hit rate, L2 hit rate, and DRAM bytes per operation. A kernel reading the same weights every decode step with a poor L2 hit rate is the signature that prefix caching or weight-resident layouts would fix; sector-level inefficiency flags uncoalesced access. Source-level analysis (--set full with line attribution) maps SASS hotspots back to your CUDA or Triton lines — this is where a single uncoalesced gather loop gets convicted with evidence.

The Command Lines That Matter

# 1. Timeline first: profile the app, skip warmup, keep it short.
nsys profile -o serve_run --capture-range=cudaProfilerApi \
  --cuda-memory-usage=true python serve.py

# ...with torch.cuda.profiler.start() / .stop() around 20 steady-state steps...

# 2. Summarize without the GUI: top kernels, top gaps.
nsys stats --report cuda_gpu_kern_sum serve_run.nsys-rep
nsys stats --report cuda_gpu_mem_time_sum serve_run.nsys-rep

# 3. Microscope on the indicted kernel only (exclusive GPU, representative input).
ncu --set full --launch-skip 5 --launch-count 1 -f -o top_kernel \
  python serve.py

Two operational notes. First, ncu replays the kernel many times (once per metric pass), so a full-model ncu run is brutally slow and often numerically fragile — always scope it with launch-skip/count to a representative step. Second, profile steady state: skip warmup iterations (JIT compilation, autotuning, KV-cache fill) with the CUDA-profiler-API capture range, or your first finding will be warmup noise that no optimization can fix.

Production Reality: The Fix Playbook

Every nsys/ncu finding maps to a small set of fixes, and senior engineers pattern-match them directly. Launch gaps across hundreds of small kernels → CUDA graphs or torch.compile with mode="reduce-overhead". H2D copies in the hot path → pinned host memory plus overlapped prefetch streams. Dataloader stalls → more workers, pinned memory, and NVIDIA DALI-style GPU-side preprocessing when the CPU is saturated. Exposed NCCL in tensor-parallel decode → overlap all-reduce with the next layer's compute (as in Megatron-style scheduling) or drop a TP rank. Low achieved occupancy with register pressure → __launch_bounds__ and re-measure. Memory-bound attention → FlashAttention or a GQA-friendly kernel, verified by the DRAM number moving, not by faith. The loop is always the same: nsys timeline, ncu roofline, one fix, re-measure — and the re-measure is non-negotiable, because GPU performance intuition is wrong often enough that every optimization ships with a before/after trace attached.

Key Takeaways

  • Profile in order: Nsight Systems timeline first (where does time go), Nsight Compute second (why is this kernel slow) — an ncu deep-dive on a 3%-of-runtime kernel is precision wasted, and nsys stats gives you top-kernels tables without opening the GUI.
  • Read gaps, not just blocks: whitespace between microsecond kernels is CPU launch overhead (fix with CUDA graphs or torch.compile reduce-overhead mode), while H2D blocks in the hot loop are a transfer bug fixed with pinned memory and overlapped prefetch on a separate CUDA stream.
  • Let the ncu roofline pick the fix: high DRAM throughput means coalesce, tile, or shrink bytes (FP8, KV-cache quantization); high SM throughput means raise intensity or switch algorithms (FlashAttention, fused Triton kernels); both low means chase occupancy and divergence first.
  • Scope ruthlessly: skip warmup with the cudaProfilerApi capture range around steady-state steps, and replay one representative kernel with --launch-skip/--launch-count — full-model ncu runs are slow, fragile, and usually measure autotuning noise.
  • Close every loop with a re-measured trace: exposed NCCL collectives get overlapped with next-layer compute, dataloader stalls get fixed host-side, and PyTorch Profiler plus the nsys SQLite export (nsys stats --format csv) make the before/after a CI-checkable artifact, not a screenshot.
