Inference

Quantization for Inference

int8, int4, AWQ, GPTQ, k-quants, NF4 — how post-training quantization reduces model size 2–4× with minimal quality loss, and how to choose the right scheme for your deployment.

Theory

1. Why Quantization Matters at Inference

LLM inference during the decode phase is overwhelmingly memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound. Every generated token requires streaming all model weights from HBM (high-bandwidth memory) into the GPU's compute cores. A 7B-parameter model in fp16 occupies ~14 GB of weights. On an A100 with ~2 TB/s memory bandwidth, the theoretical maximum throughput is:

7B params × 2 bytes/param = 14 GB weights
A100 80GB HBM bandwidth = 2,039 GB/s
Theoretical ceiling ≈ 2,039 / 14 ≈ 145 tokens/sec (weights-only bound, no compute)

For a 70B model (140 GB fp16), this drops to ~15 tok/s. In practice, real throughput is even lower (50–60% of this ceiling) due to KV cache overhead, activation memory, and compute latency. Quantization solves the root cause: it reduces the bytes per parameter. Compress to int4 (0.5 bytes/param) and a 70B model drops from 140 GB to 35 GB — pushing the bandwidth ceiling to ~58 tok/s, a 4× improvement. Add continuous batching and you can reach hundreds of tok/s on the same hardware.

2. fp16 / bf16 — The Baseline

fp16 (IEEE 754 half-precision) is the standard floating-point format for modern inference: 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits, 10 mantissa bits. It covers a dynamic range of ~1e-8 to 65,504 — sufficient for most activation values in transformer networks. bf16 (Brain Floating Point, Google) uses the same exponent width as fp32 (8 bits) but truncates the mantissa to 7 bits. This gives bf16 a wider dynamic range (~1e-38 to 3.4e38), which eliminates overflow/underflow issues in attention softmax and layer norm operations.

In practice, fp16 and bf16 inference produce outputs indistinguishable from fp32 on all standard benchmarks. The weight values in transformer models are already fairly distributed; the extra fp32 precision in the mantissa offers no measurable benefit at inference time. All major inference engines (vLLM, TGI, TensorRT-LLM) default to fp16/bf16. The only reason to use fp32 at inference is if you suspect numerical instability in a custom architecture or if you need reproducibility to the last bit.

Hardware note: NVIDIA A100 and H100 have dedicated fp16 and bf16 tensor cores. fp16 matmuls on tensor cores are roughly 2× faster than fp32. Ampere and newer architectures support both formats natively. Apple Silicon (M2/M3/M4) favors fp16; Google TPUs favor bf16.

3. int8 (W8A8) — The Safe Bet

int8 quantization maps 32-bit floating-point weights (fp32) to 8-bit integers (int8) using a per-channel or per-tensor scale factor s = max(|W|) / 127 and zero-point offset. The dequantized weight is W_dequant = s * (W_int8 - z). The principal challenge is outlier features: in most transformer models, a tiny fraction of hidden dimensions (~0.1%) have activations that are 10–100× larger than the rest. Uniform quantization of these dimensions causes large errors that cascade through attention and FFN layers.

LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022): The key innovation is mixed-precision decomposition. Matrix columns with outlier activations are kept in fp16 and computed with fp16 matmul; the remaining 99.9% of columns are quantized to int8. This hybrid approach eliminates the outlier problem while maintaining ~2× memory savings and ~1.5–2× throughput on NVIDIA Turing/Ampere tensor cores.

SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) takes a different approach: instead of isolating outliers, it smooths activation outliers into adjacent weight channels via a per-channel scaling factor. This makes both weights and activations easier to quantize uniformly — no mixed precision needed. SmoothQuant achieves W8A8 (8-bit weights AND activations) with <0.5% accuracy loss on Llama and OPT models, and nearly 2× speedup on hardware with int8 tensor core support (A100, H100).

W8A8 means both operands are int8 — weights and activations — so the matmul itself executes on int8 tensor cores. That is what separates this family from the weight-only schemes in the next section, where activations stay in fp16 and int4 weights are dequantized on the fly. Choose W8A8 when you are compute-bound at large batch sizes; choose weight-only int4 when you are memory-bound, which is the usual case at batch size 1.

4. int4 Weight-Only (W4A16 / NF4) — The Sweet Spot

int4 quantization compresses weights to 4 bits (16 storage levels), while activations remain in fp16 during computation — hence W4A16. This is the most common production scheme because it offers ~4× memory reduction with acceptable quality degradation (2–5% on most benchmarks for 70B+ models). Both dominant methods here, AWQ and GPTQ, are weight-only and group-wise (commonly a group size of 128, i.e. one scale per 128 weights along the input dimension); neither one touches activations.

AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization) — Lin et al., 2023. The core observation: not all weights matter equally. A small fraction of input channels (~0.1–1%) are salient, and salience is judged by the magnitude of the activations flowing through them — not by the magnitude of the weights. The obvious move is to keep those channels in fp16, and AWQ explicitly rejects it: the paper argues a mixed-precision weight matrix is hardware-unfriendly and kills kernel efficiency. Instead AWQ applies a per-input-channel scaling transform. A salient weight channel is multiplied by a scale s > 1 before quantization while the matching activation channel is divided by s — mathematically an identity, but the salient weights now span more of the quantization grid, so their relative rounding error shrinks.

y = W·x  ≡  y = (W·s) · (x / s)
quantize (W·s) → int4; the 1/s folds into the preceding op's output scale
s per input channel, searched on a small calibration set to minimize output MSE

The result is uniform: every weight ends up in int4, weight-only, typically group-wise with group size 128. There are no retained fp16 channels and no mixed precision anywhere in the layer — only the searched scales, which cost a fraction of a bit per weight.

AWQ is not LLM.int8(): the two start from the same observation — a tiny set of activation-outlier channels dominates the error — but act on it in opposite ways. LLM.int8() splits the matmul, keeping outlier columns in fp16 and running the remaining 99.9% in int8, so the layer really is mixed-precision. AWQ keeps a single uniform int4 format and rescales the salient channels so they survive rounding. If you catch yourself saying "AWQ keeps ~1% of weights in fp16," you are describing LLM.int8().

GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) is the other dominant weight-only scheme, and it is W4A16 as well: 4-bit weights, typically group-wise, with activations left in fp16. It never quantizes activations. Derived from OBQ, it uses approximate second-order (Hessian-based) error compensation — quantize the weight matrix column by column, and after each column update the remaining not-yet-quantized weights to absorb the rounding error just committed. This is equivalent to minimizing the L2 output error of each linear layer. GPTQ runs in a few GPU hours on 100–1000 calibration samples — no gradient descent, no retraining.

AWQ vs GPTQ tradeoff: GPTQ minimizes layer-wise L2 error with second-order information, compensating for every rounding decision as it goes — mathematically the more rigorous of the two, but it never asks which channels the activations actually stress. AWQ ignores the Hessian and instead reshapes the weight distribution so activation-salient channels round well. Both emit the same artifact: a uniform group-wise int4 weight matrix consumed by fp16 activations. In practice, AWQ-int4 and GPTQ-int4 on Llama 3 70B land within 0.5–1% MMLU of each other; the choice usually comes down to tooling, kernel support, and calibration time (AWQ's search is typically the faster pass).

NF4 (4-bit NormalFloat) — The data type used in QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023). NormalFloat is an information-theoretically optimal quantization data type for normally distributed data. Since pretrained LLM weights are approximately normally distributed, NF4 allocates quantization levels proportionally to the probability mass of a standard normal — more levels near zero, fewer in the tails. Combined with double quantization (quantizing the per-block scaling factors themselves to 8-bit), NF4 achieves ~4.5 bits/parameter memory usage while preserving quality within 0.5% of fp16 on most tasks.

5. GGUF and k-quants — The Consumer Hardware Ecosystem

GGUF is a file format, not a quantization algorithm. GGUF (GPT-Generated Unified Format) is the container used by llama.cpp and the successor to GGML's format: a single memory-mappable file holding the tensors plus metadata (architecture, hyperparameters, tokenizer, rope settings, chat template). A GGUF file will happily store fp16 tensors. "Quantized with GGUF" is a category error the way "compressed with .zip" would be — the real question is which scheme is inside the GGUF.

The algorithms inside are llama.cpp's k-quants. They are block-wise: weights are chunked into small blocks (typically 16 or 32 values) that each carry their own scale (and, for asymmetric types, a min). The _K variants add a second level — a super-block whose own scale quantizes the per-block scales, which is where most of the sub-4-bit efficiency comes from, since the metadata is compressed too. The newer IQ* types go further and use an importance matrix derived from calibration data to decide which values must be preserved.

The importance matrix — budgeting bits per row, not per tensor

The _K recipe above budgets precision per tensor: every attention projection gets Q6_K, every FFN down-projection gets Q4_K. The importance matrix (imatrix) goes one granularity finer and budgets per row. The idea: run a few hundred samples of representative text through the fp16 model, record each weight row's actual contribution to the layer output, and spend bits where the contribution is large. Rows that barely move the output drop to 2–3 bits; rows that dominate it keep 5–6 bits. The IQ* quant types (IQ2_XS through IQ4_XS) are the k-quants that consume this matrix — same GGUF container, but the packing is driven by measured importance instead of a fixed recipe.

importance(row i) = Ex ~ calibration[ ‖Wi · x‖² ]
i.e. the expected squared norm of the row's contribution to the layer output.
High ‖Wx‖² → protect at 5–6 bit. Low ‖Wx‖² → safe at 2–3 bit.

Contrast this with the static formulas elsewhere on this page: s = max(|W|) / 127 looks only at the weight values, never at what flows through them, and GPTQ's Hessian compensation minimizes reconstruction error without asking which rows the activations actually stress. The imatrix asks the activations directly — it is the calibration-set answer to "which rows would hurt most if rounded." In practice that makes IQ quants the best quality-per-byte below 4 bits: IQ4_XS roughly matches Q4_K_M quality at a smaller file, and IQ2_XS is the last stop before Q2_K survival mode.

Workflow with llama.cpp, two steps: first generate the matrix (llama-imatrix -m model.gguf -f train-data.txt -o imatrix.dat over a few hundred representative prompts), then quantize with an IQ* type that reads it (llama-quantize --imatrix imatrix.dat model.gguf model-iq4xs.gguf IQ4_XS). Two caveats. First, scales are searched, not static: the per-block scale (and min) is chosen by grid-searching candidate values to minimize reconstruction error on the calibration data — the same "search on a small calibration set" idea as AWQ's scale s in §4, applied per block. Garbage calibration domain (e.g. Wikipedia text for a medical chatbot) gives garbage scales, exactly as with GPTQ/AWQ. Second, the imatrix varies by uploader: two GGUF files with the same Q4_K_M or IQ4_XS

The other defining feature is per-tensor mixed precision: a packing recipe assigns different k-quant types to different tensors in the same model. Q4_K_M (the community sweet spot) uses:

  • Q6_K (6-bit): attention query/key/value projections — because attention is sensitive to weight precision
  • Q5_K (5-bit): feed-forward network up/gate projections
  • Q4_K (4-bit): feed-forward network down projections — larger but less sensitive

This per-tensor strategy allows Llama 3 70B to run on a single RTX 4090 24GB (with offloading) or, more practically, on a Mac Studio with 192 GB unified memory at ~12 tok/s — usable for batch processing and low-throughput applications. The other common types are Q8_0 (8-bit, effectively lossless), Q6_K, Q5_K_M, Q3_K_M (aggressive compression), and the IQ* importance-matrix quants at 1–4 bits.

6. PTQ vs QAT

Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) applies quantization to a fully trained model without any retraining. It requires a small calibration dataset (100–1000 samples) to compute scaling factors (dynamic range) or Hessian corrections (GPTQ). PTQ is fast (minutes to hours), requires no training infrastructure, and is sufficient for most production use cases at int8 and int4 precision. All methods described above (LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant, AWQ, GPTQ, the k-quants) are PTQ.

Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) inserts fake quantization nodes during training/continued pretraining, so the model learns to produce good outputs under quantized arithmetic. QAT can achieve <0.1% quality loss even at int4 — but rarely run it yourself for decoder-only LLMs, where PTQ-int4 already works well and a full retrain is expensive. Instead, prefer official -qat builds when they exist (e.g. Gemma -qat releases): a factory QAT model beats a community PTQ quant at the same size, because the weights were trained to survive the rounding rather than rounded after the fact. QAT remains standard practice in vision transformers and edge deployment (TFLite, CoreML) where every bit matters.

Localize the error before choosing the quant. Quantization loss is not uniform: measure per-layer output error (mean-squared error between the fp16 layer output and the quantized layer output on a calibration set) and you get a heatmap with a consistent shape — embedding tables and the first/last transformer layers glow, attention query/key/value projections run hotter than FFN down-projections, middle-layer FFNs stay cool. That measurement is exactly what the §5 importance matrix automates per row, and it is why the Q4_K_M recipe spends Q6_K on attention and only Q4_K on FFN downs. When a quant underperforms, do not guess: profile the per-layer error, raise the bit-width of the hottest layers first (or flag them do-not-quantize), and re-test. 8. Production Reality

vLLM

Supports AWQ, GPTQ, and SqueezeLLM quantization formats natively. The --quantization awq flag enables 4-bit inference with PagedAttention. vLLM's memory management integrates with quantized weight loading so the KV cache gets whatever VRAM remains.

llama.cpp

The defacto standard for consumer-hardware inference. GGUF container files packed with k-quants, mixing types per tensor. Runs Llama 3 70B Q4_K_M on 48GB+ VRAM or Mac Studio. Supports NVIDIA CUDA, Apple Metal, AMD ROCm, Vulkan, and pure CPU (AVX2/AVX-512/NEON).

HuggingFace TGI

Supports GPTQ, AWQ, and bitsandbytes int8 through the --quantize flag. EETQ (Easy & Efficient Transformer Quantization) integration provides optimized int8 kernels. Flash Attention + quantization work together.

TensorRT-LLM

NVIDIA's framework supports INT8 (post-training and SmoothQuant) and INT4 (AWQ, GPTQ) with custom CUDA kernels. Delivers the highest absolute throughput on A100/H100 — often 30%+ faster than vLLM for the same quantized model — but requires compilation.

MLX / mlx-lm

Apple's array framework and the mlx-lm toolkit: the native quant track on Apple Silicon. Convert with mlx-lm.convert --quantize --q-bits 4, serve 4-bit weights with an 8-bit KV path, all in unified memory with no CPU/GPU transfer split. Choose MLX when the fleet is Apple Silicon and Python-first; choose GGUF + llama.cpp when you need cross-platform (CUDA/ROCm/Vulkan) or the per-tensor K-quant recipe control of §5.

9. Hands-on: Ollama

Ollama is the fastest way to run the quants above on a laptop: one daemon, one model file, sensible defaults. A bare ollama pull llama3.1:8b fetches the default tag, which is Q4_K_M — the §5 sweet spot, not a random choice. When quality matters more than size, ask for a bigger tag explicitly:

ollama pull llama3.1:8b  # default tag = Q4_K_M
ollama pull llama3.1:8b-instruct-q8_0  # near-lossless 8-bit
ollama pull llama3.1:8b-instruct-q6_K  # middle ground for code/math
ollama show llama3.1:8b  # parameters, template, quantization level
ollama ps  # what is loaded now, VRAM/RAM footprint, context length

Two environment knobs control the memory the weights do not cover — the KV cache (see the KV-cache note 01-009 for why it matters):

OLLAMA_KV_CACHE_TYPE=q8_0  # quantize the KV cache: q8_0 (safe) or q4_0 (longer context, slight quality cost)
OLLAMA_FLASH_ATTENTION=1  # memory-efficient attention → fits longer contexts in the same RAM

The operator loop: pull Q4_K_M, check ollama ps resident size against the §7 budget (weights + overhead + KV), and only then step up to q8_0/q6_K — or step the KV cache down to q4_0 — when the context window, not the weights, is what overflows. Long-context RAG on a 16 GB laptop is usually won in these two variables, not by re-quantizing the weights.

Key Takeaways

  • Quantization is a memory-bandwidth optimization first, compute optimization second. The decode phase is memory-bandwidth-bound at real-world batch sizes. Quantization reduces the bytes-per-weight that must be streamed from HBM each token. The throughput ceiling scales linearly with compression ratio — int4 gives you ~4× the theoretical tok/s of fp16 on the same GPU, even ignoring compute savings from int4 matmuls.
  • Outlier channels are the enemy — AWQ beats them by rescaling, not by keeping fp16. In transformer models, ~0.1% of hidden dimensions have activation magnitudes 10–100× larger than average, and uniform quantization of these channels causes catastrophic errors. LLM.int8() isolates outlier columns in fp16; SmoothQuant migrates the outlier magnitude into adjacent weights. AWQ instead multiplies activation-salient weight channels by a searched scale s > 1 (dividing the matching activation by s) so they survive rounding — every weight still lands in group-wise int4. GPTQ is also weight-only W4A16 but minimizes layer-wise L2 error with Hessian compensation instead; in practice the two land within 0.5–1% MMLU on 70B models.
  • int4 on 70B+ models is production-safe — and prefer factory QAT over community PTQ. Llama 3 70B int4 (AWQ) scores ~81.5% on MMLU vs ~83.5% at fp16 — imperceptible for chat, summarization, and RAG, transformative for VRAM (140 GB → 35 GB). Below 8B parameters, int4 degrades more noticeably — stick with int8 for small models. And do not run QAT yourself: official -qat builds (e.g. Gemma) beat community PTQ at the same size because the weights trained under simulated rounding.
  • GGUF is only the container — read the tag, then run it the easy way. The quality comes from the schemes inside: block-wise k-quants with super-block scales plus per-tensor mixing (Q4_K_M: Q6_K attention, Q5_K FFN gates, Q4_K FFN downs). The tag names the recipe: default to Q4_K_M, step up to Q6_K/Q8_0 for code and math, never legacy Q4_0, and treat Q2_K as survival mode past the ~3-bit cliff. Same tag from different uploaders can still differ (different calibration corpora) — verify the exact file on your own eval set. The operator loop is Ollama: default pulls are Q4_K_M, ollama show/ollama ps inspect, and OLLAMA_KV_CACHE_TYPE + OLLAMA_FLASH_ATTENTION win long context on a laptop. On Apple Silicon, MLX (mlx-lm, 4-bit weights + 8-bit KV in unified memory) is the native alternative to GGUF.
  • NF4 + double quantization is the information-theoretic ideal for normally distributed weights. QLoRA's NormalFloat data type allocates quantization levels proportional to the probability mass of a standard normal distribution — it is provably optimal for Gaussian-distributed data. Combined with 8-bit double quantization of scaling factors, NF4 achieves ~4.5 effective bits/parameter (beating uniform int4's 4.0). For QLoRA finetuning, this means the base model fits in ~33 GB instead of ~35 GB for Llama 3 70B — small gap, but enough to matter on a 48GB GPU.
  • When 4 bits are not enough, budget bits per row and localize the error first. importance(row) = E[‖Wx‖²] over calibration data: rows that move the output keep 5–6 bits, rows that do not drop to 2–3, hottest layers get do-not-quantize flags — IQ4_XS matches Q4_K_M quality at a smaller file. Before choosing, profile per-layer output MSE on a calibration set (the heatmap view above): embeddings, early/late attention, and the final norm glow while mid-layer FFNs stay cool, which is exactly why recipes spend Q6_K on attention. Raise the hottest layers first, re-test, repeat.
