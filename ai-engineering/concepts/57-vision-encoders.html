Vision Encoders for VLMs

How images become tokens a language model can read — CLIP contrastive pretraining, the SigLIP sigmoid fix, captioning pretraining in CapPa and CoCa, and why the encoder caps everything the VLM can see.

Theory

Why the encoder is the ceiling

Every vision-language model inherits its eyes from a pretrained vision encoder, and no projector, adapter, or language backbone can recover detail the encoder discarded. If the encoder maps two different street signs to the same embedding, the LLM downstream hallucinates with full confidence — the information is gone before the first transformer layer runs. Encoder choice is therefore the highest-leverage decision in a VLM stack: it fixes the resolution grid, the pretraining biases, and the failure modes of everything built on top. This page covers the four pretraining objectives that define the modern menu — CLIP, SigLIP, CapPa, CoCa — plus the ALIGN footnote that proved scale beats curation.

CLIP: contrastive learning at batch 32,768

CLIP (OpenAI, 2021) trains an image encoder and a text encoder jointly with the InfoNCE contrastive loss: within a batch of N image-text pairs, each image must pick its own caption out of all N captions, and vice versa. The loss is a softmax over the batch, with a learned temperature sharpening the distribution:

CLIP InfoNCE: L = -log( exp(sim(i,t+)/tau) / sum_j exp(sim(i,t_j)/tau) ) SigLIP: L = -sum_{pairs} log sigmoid( z * (t*sim(i,t) - b) ), z = +1 matched / -1 else, t = learned temperature

Three consequences shaped everything after. First, the softmax couples every pair to every other pair in the batch, so CLIP needs enormous batches (32,768) for stable gradients — small-batch CLIP underperforms badly. Second, the global normalization makes distributed training communication-heavy: every worker needs every other worker's embeddings. Third, contrastive training is superb at retrieval-shaped understanding (which caption goes with which image) and weak at fine detail: the loss only asks "is this the right pair," never "describe what is in it," so encoders learn bag-of-words-ish representations that confuse counts, positions, and small text. Nearly every VLM failure at reading tiny chart labels traces back to this property of its CLIP encoder.

SigLIP: the sigmoid fix

SigLIP (Google, 2023) keeps the data and the two-tower setup but replaces the batch-softmax with an independent pairwise sigmoid loss: every image-text pair is its own binary decision — matched or not — with no normalization across the batch. Two engineering details make it work. The bias term b, initialized to −10, absorbs the massive matched-vs-mismatched imbalance (in a batch of N there are N matches against N²−N mismatches); without it, early training drowns in negatives. And because pairs decouple, the loss chunks over the batch: each worker computes its local pairs and the memory cost drops from quadratic in batch size to linear, which is also why SigLIP stays strong at small batch sizes where CLIP collapses.

The practical upshot: SigLIP-trained encoders (the So400m shape is the community default) match or beat CLIP at the same compute, train on multilingual data without batch-composition headaches, and dominate open-VLM encoder slots — Qwen-VL, PaliGemma, and LLaVA-OneVision onward ship a SigLIP encoder rather than the original CLIP. When someone says "just use SigLIP," they mean this specific combination: sigmoid loss, learned temperature and bias, chunked large-effective-batch training.

CapPa: captioning as pretraining

CapPa asks a heretical question: what if the encoder is trained by captioning — predicting the caption tokens from the image — instead of contrasting? The setup trains the vision encoder together with a text decoder from scratch, mixing two decoding modes: parallel prediction (all caption tokens at once, ~75% of training) for speed and autoregressive decoding (~25%) to keep generation coherent. Parallel prediction is the key efficiency move: it turns captioning from a slow sequential objective into one cheap enough to serve as pretraining.

The finding that matters: at matched compute, captioning-pretrained encoders beat contrastive ones on tasks that require saying what is in the image (captioning, VQA-style probing) while staying competitive on retrieval. The loss demands generative detail — to emit the caption, the encoder must preserve attributes, relations, and small objects that contrastive learning is free to ignore. CapPa never became the default encoder, but it proved the point CoCa then productized: contrastive learning retrieves, captioning describes, and the best encoder does both.

CoCa: both losses, one model

CoCa (Contrastive Captioners, Google 2022) trains a single model with a weighted sum of the contrastive and captioning losses, and its architecture is the reason the combination is affordable. The text side is a cascaded split decoder: the bottom half of the layers runs as a unimodal text encoder with no cross-attention (producing the embeddings the contrastive loss needs), while the top half adds cross-attention to the image (producing the multimodal states the captioning loss needs). One forward pass serves both losses with roughly the cost of one decoder instead of two.

On the vision side, CoCa replaces mean-pooling with attentional pooling: a bank of 256 learned queries cross-attends to the patch grid, and the pooled result becomes the single contrastive embedding, while the captioning decoder attends over the full patch sequence. And the loss combination dissolves the hardest problem in contrastive training — hard-negative mining. Pure contrastive learning on noisy web pairs needs carefully mined negatives to learn fine distinctions; CoCa's captioning loss supplies token-level generative signal on every pair, so even noisy captions teach the encoder something, and the hard-negative-vs-caption-noise tradeoff that haunts CLIP-style data curation largely disappears. CoCa-2.1B set records across retrieval, captioning, and VQA-style benchmarks simultaneously — the empirical case that joint training dominates either objective alone.

CLIP — retrieve

Batch-softmax InfoNCE, batch 32k, learned temperature. Great at matching, blind to fine detail. The incumbent every alternative is measured against.

SigLIP — retrieve, cheaper

Pairwise sigmoid plus bias init −10, chunked over the batch, robust at small batches. The default encoder in modern open VLMs.

CapPa — describe

Captioning pretraining with a parallel/autoregressive decode mix. Proves generative signal teaches detail that contrasting skips.

CoCa — both

Joint weighted loss, split cascaded decoder, 256-query attentional pooling. No hard-negative mining required; record-setting across tasks.

The ALIGN footnote: scale over curation

ALIGN trained on 1.8 billion noisily-paired images with almost no filtering — and worked. Its one-line lesson, worth remembering whenever a data-curation debate stalls: for contrastive pretraining, a 10× larger noisy dataset beats a carefully cleaned small one. Every web-scale encoder since (including the data regimes behind SigLIP and CoCa) assumes ALIGN's conclusion.

Patches, resolution, and the OCR ceiling

Whatever the loss, the encoder reads images as non-overlapping patches — 14px is the standard — so resolution directly sets the token grid: 224px yields 16×16 = 256 tokens, 336px yields 24×24 = 576, 448px yields 32×32 = 1,024. Small text dies below the patch Nyquist limit: a 10px-tall character inside a 14px patch survives as texture, not as a letter, which is why document OCR demands 448px-plus encoders while thumbnail tagging is fine at 224. And the encoder's pretraining resolution is a soft ceiling — running a 224px-pretrained encoder at 448px works, but position embeddings interpolate and fine-detail accuracy lags an encoder natively trained at the higher resolution. Production reality

Default to a SigLIP So400m-shape encoder at 384–448px for new VLM work — it is the best-tested point on the quality/compute curve, with weights and preprocessing available from Hugging Face and native support in PyTorch-based training stacks. Reach for CoCa-style joint objectives when your failure analysis shows retrieval-shaped blindness (wrong object picked confidently) rather than resolution-shaped blindness (small text unreadable) — the former is a loss problem, the latter a pixels problem, and teams routinely burn GPU months raising resolution to fix what is actually a contrastive-only representation gap. Evaluate encoders on your documents, not ImageNet zero-shot: the leaderboard that predicts chart-QA accuracy is your own eval set, and TPU-trained SigLIP variants sometimes differ from their PyTorch ports in preprocessing details (crop ratios, normalization) that shift scores by points. Freeze the encoder through projector alignment, unfreeze it late for domain adaptation — and remember the ceiling rule: once the encoder merges two signs into one embedding, no prompt engineering gets them back.

Key Takeaways

  • The encoder is a ceiling, not a feature. If SigLIP or CLIP merges two details into one embedding, no adapter, projector, or GPT-class backbone recovers them — encoder evals on your own documents outrank any leaderboard when choosing between OpenAI CLIP weights and newer SigLIP checkpoints on Hugging Face.
  • SigLIP's bias-init-minus-10 plus chunked sigmoid is what makes small-batch contrastive training work. Teams that reimplement "CLIP loss" with a softmax at batch 2k and wonder why retrieval is flat are paying the batch-coupling tax; switching to the pairwise sigmoid formulation is usually worth more than doubling the data.
  • Contrastive retrieves, captioning describes — CoCa proved you should train both. The split cascaded decoder plus 256-query attentional pooling makes the joint loss affordable, and the captioning signal removes the hard-negative mining treadmill that consumes most CLIP data-curation effort.
  • Resolution is a token budget decision with a Nyquist floor. 224/336/448px give 256/576/1,024 tokens at 14px patches, and text smaller than a patch survives only as texture — size the encoder to the smallest detail your task needs, then verify with the degradation slider above, not with ImageNet zero-shot.
  • Unfreeze late, and watch the preprocessing. Keep the encoder frozen through projector alignment, adapt it during instruction tuning, and diff preprocessing (crop ratio, normalization) when moving weights between TPU-trained releases and PyTorch training — silent preprocessing drift costs real points.
