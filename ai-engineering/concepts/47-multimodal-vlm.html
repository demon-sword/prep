Foundations

Multimodal Models & VLMs

How vision-language models turn pixels into tokens the LLM can attend to — adapters, dynamic resolution, the image-token budget, video tokens, and the early-fusion exception.

Theory

The core pattern: encoder plus projector

A vision-language model is a language model with eyes grafted on. The dominant architecture keeps two pretrained halves and glues them together: a vision encoder (almost always a CLIP- or SigLIP-trained ViT) that converts an image into a grid of patch embeddings, and a projector/adapter that maps those embeddings into the language model's token space. The image then arrives at the LLM as an ordinary prefix of "tokens" — typically 32 to 1,024 of them — and the transformer attends over them exactly as it would over words. Nothing inside the LLM's attention math changes; the entire trick is in how faithfully the adapter preserves visual detail while spending as few sequence positions as possible.

Training follows the LLaVA two-stage recipe that nearly every open VLM still copies. Stage one freezes both the encoder and the LLM and trains only the projector on image-caption pairs, aligning the two embedding spaces. Stage two unfreezes the LLM (and sometimes the encoder) and instruction-tunes on multimodal conversations. Skipping stage one and training everything jointly from scratch sounds simpler, but in practice the unaligned projector injects noise gradients into the LLM and collapses its language ability early — alignment first, instruction-following second.

The adapter taxonomy: four ways to cross the modality gap

Adapters fall on a spectrum from "keep every pixel" to "compress to agist". Each point on it is a real system, and each makes a different token-budget tradeoff.

MLP projector (LLaVA)

A two-layer MLP maps each patch embedding to LLM dimension, one token per patch. Zero compression: a 336px CLIP image yields 576 tokens, a 448px tile yields 1,024. Maximum fidelity for document OCR and chart reading; maximum context cost.

Q-Former (BLIP-2)

32 learned query vectors cross-attend to the frozen image features. Pretrained in two stages with three objectives: ITC (contrastive alignment), ITM (fine-grained matching), ITG (generation). Output is always 32 tokens no matter the input resolution — a 32× compression of a 1,024-patch tile. Qwen-VL's single-layer cross-attention adapter is the same idea: 448px tiles compressed to 256 queries each.

Perceiver resampler + gated cross-attention (Flamingo)

A perceiver resampler compresses thousands of visual features into a small bank of learned latents (64 in Flamingo), and the frozen LLM reads them through newly added gated cross-attention layers — gated so they initialize to the identity and cannot damage the pretrained language behavior. Nothing visual is ever concatenated into the text sequence: the token budget cost is zero positions, paid instead in extra parameters and attention FLOPs at every layer.

Cross-attention adapters (LLaMA-3.2) → early fusion (LLaMA-4)

LLaMA-3.2 keeps the encoder but weaves cross-attention adapter layers into the LLM backbone at every fourth block, so vision conditions generation deep inside the network rather than only as a prefix. LLaMA-4 goes one step further and drops the separate encoder entirely: native early fusion, where image patches are tokenized and fed to a single unified transformer from the first layer.

The interview-level summary: prefix adapters (MLP, Q-Former) spend context positions; cross-attention adapters (Flamingo, LLaMA-3.2) spend parameters and per-layer FLOPs instead; early fusion (LLaMA-4, Chameleon below) spends pretraining compute by forcing one backbone to learn both modalities from scratch.

Resolution: tiling, and why NaViT matters

Encoder pretraining fixes a native resolution — 224, 336, or 448 pixels — but real images are larger and stranger-shaped. The standard answer is tiling: slice the image into native-resolution tiles, encode each independently, and concatenate the tokens. With the ubiquitous 14px patch size, one 448px tile produces (448/14)² = 1,024 tokens. A 1344×896 document scan needs 3×2 = 6 tiles, or 6,144 tokens before any adapter compression. Resolution costs scale quadratically, which is why the resolution slider in the visualization below climbs so fast.

NaViT attacked the two inefficiencies hiding inside tiling. First, square-padding waste: padding a 16:9 photo up to a square throws away 1 − 9/16 ≈ 44% of the encoded positions on padding. Second, batch rigidity: fixed resolutions force every image in a batch through the same grid. NaViT packs images at their native aspect ratios into one sequence (sequence packing, like document packing for text) and uses factorized position embeddings so each patch still knows where it sits. Qwen2-VL adopted exactly this dynamic-resolution scheme with M-RoPE positions, which is how open models read arbitrarily shaped screenshots without the padding tax. The token budget: images eat contexts

In document-heavy workloads the image tokens dominate everything else. One 448px tile at full MLP projection costs 1,024 tokens — roughly five times the ~200 text tokens that a page of OCR-extracted text would cost. A ten-page PDF at two tiles per page is ~20,000 image tokens before a single word of instructions. This is the economic force behind the whole adapter taxonomy: Q-Former-style 32-token compression turns that 20k into ~640, at the price of fine print the queries chose not to keep. Retrieval-heavy applications over slides and screenshots live or die on this tradeoff, which is why production systems (Qwen-VL, DeepSeek-VL, Kimi-VL) all ship learned resamplers rather than raw patch projection.

tokens(image) = tiles × (tile_px / patch_px)² × adapter_keep_rate MLP: keep = 1.0 → 1,024 tokens per 448px tile Q-Former: 32 queries → keep ≈ 0.03 → 32 tokens per tile Perceiver (Flamingo): 64 latents, 0 concatenated positions

Video tokens: frames times tokens-per-frame

Video is the same problem multiplied by time. The naive scheme concatenates per-frame tokens: total = frames × tokens-per-frame, so a 60-second clip sampled at 1 frame per second with 256 tokens per frame costs 15,360 tokens — a full small context window for one minute of video. Three standard reductions stack on top. Frame-rate sampling (1–2 FPS for most open video models) exploits temporal redundancy: little changes between adjacent frames at 30 FPS. Conv3D tube patches (2×16×16 instead of 1×16×16) fold short motion into the patch embedding itself, halving the frame count before tokenization. Token merging and run-length schemes drop near-duplicate patches across consecutive frames, spending tokens only where pixels actually changed. The visualization's video mode computes the bill live: raise the frame rate and watch a two-minute clip exceed the context before the model reads a single word.

The exception: Chameleon and early fusion

Chameleon is the deliberate counterexample to everything above. Instead of a continuous-signal encoder plus adapter, it quantizes images with a VQ-VAE tokenizer into 1,024 discrete tokens per image and trains a single transformer from scratch on mixed-modal sequences where text and image tokens interleave freely. There is no adapter because there are no two spaces to align — one vocabulary, one backbone, token-level modeling from layer zero. The price is the entire pretraining bill: no frozen CLIP, no frozen LLM, and mixed-modal data at a scale only a handful of labs can afford. When an interviewer asks "why doesn't everyone do early fusion," the answer is that grafting (encoder plus adapter) reuses hundreds of millions of dollars of pretrained capability, while early fusion repays that debt in training compute for full architectural freedom.

Production reality

Three rules survive contact with serving. First, match the adapter to the task's detail density: document OCR, chart QA, and screenshot agents need full-grid MLP projection at high tiling; vibe-captioning and thumbnail retrieval are fine on 32-query compression. Second, unfreeze the encoder late, not early: stage-one alignment with a frozen encoder is stable, and unfreezing mid-instruction-tuning adapts the vision features to your domain without the early-training collapse. Third, budget image tokens before text prompts: with Qwen-style or Llama-style serving stacks, a user uploading five phone photos at full tiling can silently consume 10k+ context tokens, so production gateways downscale, cap tiles per image, and report the image-token count back — the number this page's counter computes is the number your router should enforce.

Key Takeaways

  • Prefix adapters spend positions, cross-attention adapters spend FLOPs. LLaVA-style MLP projection and Q-Former queries concatenate visual tokens into the sequence (up to 1,024 per 448px tile raw, 32 compressed); Flamingo and Llama-style gated cross-attention keep the sequence clean but pay per-layer attention cost — pick by whether your bottleneck is context length or latency, as Qwen and DeepSeek serving configs do explicitly.
  • Resolution cost is quadratic and tiling multiplies it. (448/14)² = 1,024 tokens per tile is the number to memorize; a 6-tile document scan is 6k tokens before compression, which is why production VLM gateways cap tiles per image and downscale phone photos before they ever reach the Hugging Face pipeline.
  • NaViT packing recovers up to ~44% padding waste on wide images by encoding native aspect ratios instead of square-padding, and Qwen2-VL proved the dynamic-resolution scheme works at scale with M-RoPE positions — any system reading screenshots or video frames should pack, not pad.
  • Video budgets are frames × tokens-per-frame — do the multiplication first. 60s at 1 FPS with 256 tokens per frame is 15,360 tokens; Conv3D tube patching and cross-frame token merging are not optional refinements but the difference between a clip fitting in context or not, and PyTorch video models now ship tube patching as the default path.
  • Chameleon-style early fusion is architecturally cleanest and economically hardest: one discrete vocabulary and no adapter, but no frozen CLIP and no frozen LLM to lean on. Graft when you need results this quarter on Llama or Qwen backbones; fuse early only when you own the pretraining budget.
